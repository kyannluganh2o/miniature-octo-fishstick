A B S T R A C T
In liquid-fueled detonations droplets are subjected to a myriad of complex codependent physical phenomena
occurring on overlapping temporal and spatial scales, resulting in rapid vaporization. The rate at which droplets
vaporize is enhanced by the concurrent hydrodynamic breakup processes. This article describes experiments
where small ( 𝑑 < 125 μm ) droplet breakup at high Weber number 𝑂(104–105) is imaged in a self-sustained
liquid-fueled detonation with laser optical Mie scattering imagery. Experimental initial conditions, including
the droplet size and mass distribution, are characterized and reported. Child droplet clouds generated from
droplet interactions with detonation waves are observed to persist for approximately 10 mm past the detonation
front and grow to the order of millimeters in width. A velocity deficit of ∼ 10% was observed for the
multiphase detonations wave speed when compared to calculations for the equivalent gaseous detonations.
The bulk droplet survival distances and breakup cloud morphology are compared to the predictions of
relevant evaporation and breakup models. Calculations indicate that evaporation alone would result in droplet
survival distances orders of magnitude longer than those observed. A droplet process whereby breakup occurs
over an extended time, concurrent with evaporation, provides a phenomenological explanation. Empirical
models constructed for shock-driven breakup predicted larger child droplet sizes than theoretical models
based on linear stability theory, yielding survival distances and cloud shapes within the range of values
seen in experiments. Droplets were however observed to persist longer than either model would predict. The
discrepancy between calculations and experiment indicate that development of models tailored to droplets
subject to variable acceleration are necessary to fully explain the multiphase detonation.
1. Introduction
The pressures, temperatures, and gas velocities generated by a det-
onation may be harnessed for a variety of purposes. Detonation-driven
propulsion, in devices such as rotating and pulse detonation engines,
is an application of particular interest. In practical applications these
devices may be implemented as liquid-fueled air-breathing engines [1],
operating on aviation fuels, e.g. heavy hydrocarbons C10-C16+. [ 2],
with fuel introduced in the form of droplets and vapor. A question
of particular interest is the efficiency of a multiphase detonation at
extracting the available chemical energy of the fuel, in comparison to
gaseous detonations. One metric of comparison between the two is the
detonation velocity deficit between the multiphase and ideal gaseous
systems. Development of such devices requires an understanding of the
mechanisms and characteristics of droplet processing in detonations. A
liquid droplet in such an environment is exposed to extreme acceler-
ations, temperatures, and pressures, regimes where the behavior has
not been characterized. Similar physical regimes of high temperatures
and complex accelerations exist in other systems, such as a liquid jet
injected into a supersonic crossflow of a scramjet combustor [ 3] or

Distinct droplet breakup regimes have been described in the liter-
ature [6]. The high 𝑊 𝑒 regime ( > 103) of interest here is sometimes
described as the catastrophic regime, where waves of various size strip
child droplets from the surface (Kelvin–Helmholtz type) and the parent
undergoes large scale deformation and bulk breakup (Rayleigh–Taylor
type). The work of Pilch and Erdman [ 6] was extended with various
studies, such as Widdecke et al. [ 7] and Ranger and Nicholls [ 8],
investigating shock-driven breakup at higher Mach numbers, up to
about 3, and Weber numbers over 1000. Waldmann and Reinecke [ 9]
were able to conduct a number of studies in the 1970s, investigating
millimeter size droplets interacting with shocks ranging from Mach 2–
11, and accompanying high ( 104) Weber numbers. Here they observed
catastrophic breakup regimes and near-immediate droplet demise, pro-
viding empirical correlations for breakup behavior in those extreme
environments. The diagnostics of the era were limited in temporal
and spatial resolution, limiting what could be inferred about droplet
morphology or time evolution of the breakup process. The literature
is otherwise notably sparse for droplet breakup occurring at high ( >
104) 𝑊 𝑒 with relatively small (< 100 μm) droplets and strong (Mach 6-
8+) accelerations, regimes more relevant to liquid-fueled detonations.
The literature is also lacking in experiments specifically investigating
the behavior of droplets interacting with a complex shock & expansion
acceleration profile, like that produced by a detonation wave or about
a hypersonic vehicle traveling in atmosphere.
In liquid-fueled detonations, the breakup phenomena quickly be-
comes more complex than the inert, shock-driven case. Detonations, in
the simplest 1D explanation, consist of a shock wave with a coupled
induction and reaction zone of finite width which terminates at a
sonic plane, followed by an adiabatic expansion region, as described by
Zeldovich–Neumann–Doring (ZND) theory [11–13]. The leading shock
is supported by energy release from the reactions up to the sonic
plane, known as the Chapman–Jouget or CJ point. In 2D and 3D the
phenomena becomes more complex, with transverse waves forming and
interacting, leading to the characteristic detonation cell structure wit-
nessed on soot foils (cell size often denoted by𝜆). Detonation properties

The acceleration, temperatures, and pressures that a fuel droplet
would see in the induction and reaction region vary, with a droplet
seeing rapid acceleration and heating. Weber number in a polydisperse
droplet spray may vary by several orders of magnitude, both due to
droplet size and transient gas velocities. Fig. 1 illustrates 𝑊 𝑒 variations
due gas property variations in the region between the wave front
and CJ point for a gaseous 1D ZND detonation for droplet diameters
in the range of interest to this study. The 𝑊 𝑒 presented is that of
the initial droplet immediately behind the shock wave, e.g. that of a
stationary, non-deformed droplet at its initial temperature and mass. A
droplet evaporating and breaking up would see further changes in 𝑊 𝑒,
including a possible increase due to heating and a corresponding drop
in surface tension and decrease due to shrinkage and breakup. Child
droplets generated from breakup could feasibly fall from the high We,
catastrophic (> 103) regime into one of the lower We, classical breakup
regimes.
In order for a detonation to propagate, vaporization of the droplets
is necessary to provide fuel in a gaseous phase which may react and
release energy. Reaction rates are limited by available fuel vapor to re-
act, thus the system is coupled in that regard. Droplets subject to strong
shock waves will naturally begin to evaporate due to high post-shock
temperatures and convection, a process enhanced by the temperatures
generated in combustion. Evaporation of the parent drop, even at high
temperatures, occurs at much slower time scales than the detonation,
necessitating that some other process occur to enhance vaporization.
Recent simulation work [ 14] proposes that droplet breakup is a suffi-
cient and necessary mechanism to enhance vapor production and allow
liquid-fueled detonations to propagate. The exact mechanism and rates
of deformation and breakup in detonation regimes, though, remains
unclear; the rates of deformation, and size and rates of child droplet
shedding are not well studied for conditions of high temperatures and
high velocities or Weber numbers, much less for variable-acceleration
conditions in the freestream about the droplet.
A handful of experimental studies into liquid-fueled detonations
have been reported. The earliest was a series of comprehensive studies
by Dabora et al. [ 15] in the 1960s and 1970s, with related work by
Lu [16]. These experiments detonated poly and nearly monodisperse
sprays of heavy hydrocarbon droplets in oxygen. The droplet diameters
used were relatively large, between 290–2600 μm. Notably, the reported
detonation velocities showed deficits of 5%–30% from the gaseous
CJ values. In the early 1990s Papavassiliou et al. [ 17] performed a
study with relatively small (5–10 μm) decane droplets and varying
equivalence ratio and O2 /inert gas compositions, though the droplet
size was not measured directly. Detonation velocities in this study were
found to be within 10% of the calculated CJ value. Detonation cell
sizes obtained from soot-foils of heterogeneous (droplet) detonations
were found to be roughly twice the size of those in the homogeneous
(gaseous) detonation. From the cell sizes it was estimated that the
induction length for the liquid-fueled detonations would be about five
times longer than that of the homogeneous, gaseous case, indicat-
ing a delay due to some physical processes leading to vaporization.
Benmahammed et al. [ 18,19] investigated liquid-fueled detonations
with an apparatus similar to Papavassiliou et al. with several fuels
(heptane, octane, decane, dodecane) and larger droplet diameters, ∼
8–45 μm . They observed that droplet diameter was a limiting factor
in detonability, and did not observe detonations for large droplets
(> 30 μm ) with less volatile fuels such as dodecane. Velocity deficits
in this study were reported as being ∼ 10%. These results contrast with
those of Dabora et al. where detonations were achieved with droplets
of similar composition and orders of magnitude larger in diameter. A
summary of the fuels, droplet sizes, and observed CJ velocity deficits
is provided in Table 1.

will occur. A central question is how the droplet breakup alters the
rate at which a liquid droplet is converted to vapor. Though the two
processes cannot be decoupled in this environment, measurements of

In this article experimental data is presented with an analysis guided
by the predictions of several extant breakup and evaporation models.
The facility and discussions of methodology and techniques are out-

2. Experimental facility and methods
2.1. Experimental facility
The facility presented is designed for investigating liquid-fueled
detonations at relatively ( < 100 μm ) small droplet sizes using modern
optical diagnostics beyond those that were previously available or
employed. The dimensions were specifically chosen to be suitable for
generating detonations of oxygen and liquid n-dodecane and other
heavy hydrocarbon fuels of interest such as decane. The detonation
tube has a 57 mm × 57 mm square cross section and is approximately
3 meters in length. The length was set to provide sufficient time for a
nominally stable detonation to form based on previous literature [17].
The diameter was selected from available nominal sizes of tubing to
allow at least seven detonation cells [20] to form, based on measure-
ments from Papvasilliou et al. [17]. A square cross section was chosen
to allow the use of flat windows for easier, undistorted optical access
in the test sections. The facility was built in a vertical orientation so
that droplets would flow down the length of the tube either naturally
or with a coflowing gas, in the preferential direction of gravity. Even
in the vertical configuration, the literature indicates that wall wetting
by spray generation devices (droplets sticking to the walls) has been
an ongoing issue to be for these types of facilities [15,17,21]. A more
thorough discussion of wall wetting is included in Appendix A.1.
The detonation tube consists of several modular sections. A 500 mm
long, 60 mm diameter round tube serves as an Ignition/DDT section.
The necessity of Deflagration-to-Detonation-Transition (DDT) devices
for liquid hydrocarbon detonation was noted for previous multiphase
studies [15,17,18]. A Shchelkin coil [22] is used to accelerate DDT
of a gaseous fuel-oxygen mixture, ignited by a spark from a high
voltage circuit. The dimensions of the Shchelkin coil in the DDT section
were chosen with correlations for blockage ratio (obstructed area ratio)
from [20]. At the end of the tube a flange is mounted with ports
for the pneumatic valves that control the gas co-flow and propane-
oxygen gas slug, as well the spark ignitor. A spray generation device
(see below) is housed in the section immediately downstream of the
DDT section. Following this is 1.5 meters of development sections
(three 500 mm sections) where the detonation transitions to the liquid-
fueled mixture and is allowed to stabilize. Optical measurements are
taken in the two test sections. The windows are quartz glass with a
viewing area of approx. 30 x 100 mm. The detonation is dispelled into
an expansion tank filled with inert gas (CO2) attached to the end of
the tube, quenching the detonation and expanding the wave to avoid
re-shock pressures. The expansion tank is separated from the other
tube sections by a optically transparent thin (0.002" 𝑖𝑛) polycarbonate
diaphragm placed between two flanges. The diaphragm is held in place
with hydraulic rams allowing for it to be easily replaced for each
experiment. Further details of the design and construction methodology
can be found in [23].
The experimental procedure was automated via a series of solenoid
and pneumatic valves controlled by an NI-PXIe system and accompa-
nying Labview program. This system also handles recording transducer
data at a maximum rate of 2 MS/s/ch and timing digital voltage signals
to trigger the laser and camera diagnostics. Pressure transducers (PCB
102B04, resolution 0.02 psi, rise time ≤ 1 μs) are spaced at regular
intervals along the length of the development sections and downstream
of the test section. These allow for measurement of detonation speeds
along the length of the development sections and across the test sec-
tions, and provide a detonation wave time-of-arrival trigger signal for
the optical diagnostics.
Sprays were generated via a SUJ11 air-blast nozzle from Spraying
Systems Company. This device generates a spray through an internal
pressure-driven mixing, supplied with oxygen from the facility gas man-
ifold and liquid n-dodecane fuel through a pressurized sample cylinder.
The nozzle body was placed inside the tube along the center axis with
the spray tip pointing downward in the direction of gravity. Operating
parameters of the nozzle were able to be tuned to deliver various
droplet sizes and mass flow rates. A co-flow of oxygen introduced from
the end of the tube is used to aid in the transport the smaller droplets
(faster settling time than from to gravity alone) and maintain the size
distribution. The coflowing gas was vented from a valve at the bottom
of the tube, to generate a steady flow of gas along the length. The
desired droplet sizes and flow rate were achieved using a 10 psig liquid
pressure (approx. 0.65 g/s liquid flow rate) and a 5 psig gas pressure
(approx. 9 lpm gas flow rate), with a 25 lpm oxygen co-flow. The
orientation of the gas co-flow, spray, and imaging apparatus (discussed
in the next section) is displayed in Fig. 2.
2.2. Diagnostics

measured using a Phase Doppler Particle Analyzer (PDPA) system and
accompanying TSI FlowSizer software. In this study, the PDPA integra-

droplets were measured at the same conditions as the detonation initial

equipped with a 300 mm lens capable of measuring droplets in the
range of 0.5–150 μm in diameter with an accuracy of ±1.5 μm for the
smallest detectable particle and ±3 μm for the largest [24]. The PDPA
system is subject to some amount of systemic error based on optical
calibration and settings. Manufacturer best practices were followed to

2.3. Methods
The experimental procedure is detailed as follows, with annota-
tions in Fig. 2 for illustration. The polycarbonate film diaphragm is
first placed between the tube and expansion tank and hydraulic rams
actuated to seal the tube and secure the diaphragm in place. The
expansion tank is evacuated to vacuum and then filled with carbon
dioxide at one atmosphere to prevent detonation from propagating
into the expansion tank. The rest of the tube is then taken to vacuum
and filled with oxygen at atmospheric pressure and temperature; this
process is repeated twice to purge any remaining atmosphere from the
tube. A steady co-flow of oxygen is then introduced to the tube. The
volumetric flow rate is metered with an orifice, and a valve downstream
of the test sections is opened to atmospheric pressure, allowing for
a steady flow to develop through the tube. Fuel droplets are then
introduced at the droplet injection site, with the mass flow rate and size
distribution controlled by pre-set fuel and air pressures to the nozzle. A
steady liquid-fuel concentration is achieved within 30 s by this method,
determined by light extinction measurements.
A propane-oxygen detonation is then used to ignite the multiphase
detonation. Properties for propane-oxygen detonations (CJ pressure,
velocity, density at various ER) are similar to detonation properties
in n-dodecane and oxygen such that they can be tuned to yield the
necessary strength to ignite the multiphase detonation. A pressurized
gas slug of rich (ER of 2) propane-oxygen mixture is introduced to the
DDT section of the tube 5 s before ignition. Once expanded to the initial

overdrive the detonation.
2.4. Imaging
In the experiments droplets interacting with the detonation were
imaged by Planar Laser Mie Scattering. A laser pulse ( < 10 ns, 160
mJ) from a Nd:YAG laser (532 nm) was expanded into a sheet (ap-
prox. 6.5 mm thick and 55 mm across) with a cylindrical lens and
directed into the tube from a window in the bottom of the expansion
tank, represented in Fig. 5. Scattered light from the droplets was then
captured with a TSI Powerview 29 MP 12 bit CCD camera mounted
normal to the laser sheet. The camera was coupled with an Infinity
Optics K2 DistaMax long distance microscope lens with a STD objective,
yielding a depth of field of 1.1 mm and a resolution of 12.1 μm per
pixel. The calibration was performed on a calibration target with a
distance between lines of 15.875 mm, corresponding to approximately
1300 pixels. The edges of the calibration lines could be found with an
accuracy of ±2 pixels, or approximately 24.2 μm. The exposure of the
CCD sensor was limited by hardware to 8 μs, though the laser pulse
illuminating the particles was much shorter, < 10 ns.
A 50 mm 532 ± 2 nm bandpass filter was used to block broadband
light emission from combustion that would otherwise leave streaks
from combustion emissions in the image. The camera and laser were
triggered with a signal from the pressure transducer closest to the test
sections (675 mm from the imaging field of view) with timing offsets
handled by the NI control system. The experiments were found to be
repeatable enough that the delay between the trigger and image could
be set as a constant value based on previous experiments.

The Mie-Scattering imagery allowed for observation of the mor-
phology of droplets through the detonation-driven breakup process. In
Fig. 6, the leading shock wave can be observed as a sharp distortion in
the image. After the shock passage, bright objects much larger than
the measured parent droplet sizes are observed to form. These are
proposed to be clouds of child droplets generated as the parent droplets
deforms and break up. The clouds grow in time until eventually the
child droplets will a reach a point where they evaporate and disappear,
ceasing to yield any strong scattering emission from the incident laser
light. The raw image intensities ranged from 0–300, with background
noise levels < 8. The mean pixel intensity in the preshock region is 5.
The image intensities are scaled in Fig. 6 to increase contrast.
By this method the history of the droplet processing is observed,
from the shock wave to the final extinction of liquid mass that may
appreciably scatter any incident laser light. Mie scattered intensity
decreases approximately proportional to surface area [ 30] until ap-
proximately 𝑑𝑝 𝜆𝑖, where 𝜆𝑖 is the incident light wavelength (here
532 nm). Below this size scattering enters the Rayleigh regimes, with
scattered intensity decreasing rapidly (see Fig. A.3 in Appendix A.4 ).
The formation of child droplets increases the net scattering as the total
surface area increases by the inverse of average droplet diameter [ 30]
in the droplet cloud. For example, the breakup of a droplet at the
D32 diameter of 40 μm into a cloud of 1 μm child droplets should
have a total scattered total intensity that is approximately 40 times
greater. Other factors may contribute to this increased intensity as well,
including deformation and secondary scattering. Thus, the resulting
droplet clouds may be both larger and possess an increased total
intensity.
The integrated Mie Scattering signal can be taken to provide a
measure of the furthest extent that a significant amount of liquid mass
from the initial parent droplets remains. This method does not however
provide quantitative data on droplet sizes and counts. Owing to the
limitations of the camera resolution, the images provide an integrated
signal that decreases as the collection of child droplets evaporate,
decrease in size, and enter the Rayleigh scattering regime. For child
droplets below 0.4 μm, it is estimated that scattered intensity is equal to
or less than that of the parent droplet source. At this size, the estimated
droplet lifetime is negligible, and it can be assumed to evaporate nearly
instantly.
Bulk droplet survival times and distances were determined quan-
titatively by processing the images to find the scattered light intensity
profile. Image intensity was integrated in the𝑥-direction (perpendicular
to the direction of shock transit) to yield a 1D signal. This signal
was then filtered for noise with a 2nd order Savitsky-Golay filter. The
bounds of the droplet breakup region were determined by fitting a
Gaussian to the signal and applying an intensity threshold. A Gaussian
fit was chosen as the intensity signal followed the form of a symmetric
exponential rise and decay. To isolate the droplet breakup and evapora-
tion region, the image was adjusted with a floor at the average intensity
of the quiescent pre-shock droplet region, as determined by a moving
average filter. The Gaussian profile was then fitted and a threshold
value of 1∕𝑒2 of the peak value was used to define the bounds of the
droplet survival region. As the flow is inhomogeneous, some amount of
variation in the measured distance can be ascribed to random spatial
fluctuations in droplet size and number density. The result of this
spatial variation can be seen in the trailing edge of the droplet cloud
in Fig. 6, where no clear boundary can be seen. The integration in the
𝑧-direction accounts for this variation by considering the total intensity
along the cross section. The bounds detected by this method are plotted
for the data in Fig. 6 . The integrated intensity, fit, and bounds of the
droplet survival region for this representative trial are presented in
Fig. 7. The average length of this region was found to be 10.88 mm with
a standard deviation of 0.52 mm (4.8%). The droplet survival distances
for each trial in this study are are provided in Table A.1 .

smaller than even the smallest clouds observed in the experiments.
3.2. Interpretation of experimental observations
3.2.1. Evaporation and breakup models
It is desired to provide a theoretical explanation the experimental
observations of droplet survival and cloud morphology in order to
relate the data to the underlying physics. This has been done here
by using the predictions of relevant breakup and evaporation models
as a guide. To this end a 0D explicit time marching routine was
implemented numerically. This scheme utilizes the models referenced
below to predict the mass loss and cloud size of a single droplet (treated
as a point particle of some diameter and mass) subject to an infinite
free stream gas flow as it advects forward in time. The conditions
of the freestream are constant at preset values of a calculated post
shock condition (rather than time-dependent detonation conditions).
The algorithm and description is provided in Appendix A.5 . The exact
formulations of drag, deformation, breakup, and vaporization models
used are detailed in full in the appendices of [14]. Coupling is one-way,
with only the droplet properties being affected by the external flow.
It is important to note that these calculations utilize a combination of
0D models, and not a full numerical simulation solving conservation
equations in a discretized domain.

3.2.2. Theoretical description of breakup and evaporation processes
The KH-RT mechanisms are posited to be the most theoretically
sound explanation for droplet breakup, though in this case the Beale
and Reitz model [33] did not predict the experimental observations
using the standard model coefficients. The empirical WERT49 model
better represents the observations in the experiments, though this
model was calibrated for lower 𝑊 𝑒 regimes. It is proposed that the
larger RT droplets are responsible for the observed cloud morphol-
ogy. KH droplets, on the other hand, are predicted to be < 0.1μm,
a size which vaporizes nearly instantaneously ( < 0.1 μs ). Thus, these
droplets do not travel a significant distance from the parent before
being extinguished.
The WERT49 model approximates RT breakup with a single parent
droplet breaking up into a group of equally sized child droplets over
a finite breakup time. Under detonation conditions the child droplets
will undergo additional breakup events, as the Weber number remains
high due to the high relative gas velocity and decreasing surface tension
due to droplet heating. As breakup progresses, child droplets are ejected
with a radial velocity by the rapidly deforming parent droplet, resulting
in expansion of the cloud width. The radial velocity of the child droplets
is predicted by the deformation rate of the parent droplet and energy
conservation as presented in the Taylor Analogy Breakup model [39].
A notional depiction of this process presented in Fig. 9, where an ex-
panding cloud of increasingly small droplets is accelerated by the flow
through time, until a point where the cloud reaches a maximum size. At
this point the child droplets do not breakup further and eventually go
extinct through convective heating and vaporization. Fig. 10 illustrates
this behavior, where much of the initial mass of the droplet is conserved
in the isolated child droplet clouds until rapid vaporization near the
extinction time. The detonation velocity deficit observed in experiments
may be explained by these two observations; as the production of vapor
is delayed until late in the breakup process, available fuel to propagate
the detonation is also delayed. As the fuel is suspended as discrete
droplets in clouds, not uniformly in the flow field, some transport and
mixing phenomena must occur over a finite time scale in order for it
to mix with gaseous oxidizer. The ready availability of vaporized fuel
is thus delayed in both space and time.

a droplet exposed to a variable acceleration would deform and break
up in a qualitatively canonical manner, however the deformation rates
and breakup morphology observed were outside of those predicted by
a normal post-shock analyses, namely with longer breakup times and
larger deformations. Models for high Weber number breakup remain
poorly developed. The current literature is otherwise sparse on the
subject of high Weber number and variable acceleration droplet defor-
mation, breakup, and evaporation. In a detonation, through the shock,
CJ point, and post shock expansion, conditions are varying rapidly,
and the structure of the flow field is three-dimensional and complex.
Simulations such as [ 14] suggest the possible structure of this flow
field, consisting of non-uniform eddies of various size behind the shock
front, in which fuel vapor or small droplets may be entrained. While the
models used here have several limitations, they provide support for the
proposed theoretical description of droplet breakup and evaporation in
detonations; child droplets produced by the KH mechanism are small
and evaporate rapidly while those produced from RT mechanisms are
larger and persist in time, spreading radially and axially.

4. Conclusion
Self-sustaining liquid-fueled detonations were created and stud-
ied experimentally in a new facility. The facility and its operation
were described in detail along with the measurement methods for
initial conditions. PDPA measurements of droplet size distribution were
taken in-situ, along with mass retention methods for equivalence ratio.
A 10% deficit in wave speed from the gaseous 1D theory was observed
for the multiphase n-Dodecane & Oxygen detonations. Child droplet
clouds resulting from detonation-driven breakup of small droplets ( <
100 μm) were imaged for the first time by laser Mie-Scattering. Imagery
provided data on the survival distance of droplets in the detonation.
The morphology of the resulting child droplet clouds in detonations dif-
fered qualitatively from that of shock-driven droplet breakup with inert
droplets, resulting in oblate shaped droplet clouds oriented laterally to
the flow.
A phenomenological explanation of the progression of the breakup
and evaporation process is presented, utilizing available theoretical
models and numerical calculations to guide the interpretation of the
experimental results with relevant physics. The droplet clouds observed
in the experiments are proposed to be a product of droplet breakup
described by shear and acceleration instabilities. Concurrent heat trans-
fer and evaporation contributes to mass loss, however this effect is
significantly pronounced only as the child droplet size decreases below
∼ 5 μm . It is proposed that droplets produced by the KH mechanism
are small and rapidly evaporate. Larger droplets are produced by RT
mechanisms and persist in time, spreading radially and axially. This
disparity in the size and rate of child droplet generation would account
for the generation of a cloud much larger in size than the initial parent
droplet diameter. A deficit in bulk detonation wave speed results due
to low initial vapor production rates due to relatively slow evaporation
rate of the parent droplets, and the heterogeneous distribution of fuel,
requiring additional time for mixing. Heat transfer, vaporization and
mixing are both enhanced by the increased surface area from the
breakup process. As the parent droplet breaks up into smaller child
droplets, vapor production is enhanced. Such a mechanism serves to
delay the combustion process.
Cloud shape data and survival times predicted by the models were
found to correlate to the range of observations of experiments. Impor-
tantly, the WERT49 model predicted larger child droplets and longer
breakup distances than the KHRT model, closer to what was observed
in the experiments. This finding indicates that large-scale breakup is oc-
curring and the process is not entirely dominated by KH mass stripping.
The predictions for breakup and vaporization were still faster than ob-
served. For the largest expected droplet size vaporization was predicted
to occur 11 mm behind the shock front, however clouds were observed
to extend further than 15 mm away. This discrepancy is believed to
stem from how the model is constructed, namely on the assumption of
constant post shock conditions. The variable conditions behind the det-
onation front, namely decelerating flow, are not accounted for. Further
development of high Weber number variable-acceleration deformation,
breakup, and evaporation models will be necessary to better predict the
evolution of droplets under these complex conditions.

A.1. Discussion of wall wetting
The observation of wall wetting necessitates a conversation on film

the findings of this study. Film detonation occurs when a shock trav-

a combustion region in a boundary layer expanding form the walls of
a tube, lagging some 5–7 tube diameters behind the leading shock. It
is also noted that, even for strong initiators, film detonations required
some 20–30 tube diameters of length to transition into a detonation.
