ABSTRACT
The growing interest of liquid-fueled detonation engines for the next-generation propulsion applications necessitates extensive understanding
of the breakup physics of droplets induced by detonation waves. However, current knowledge of this topic still remains very limited. The
breakup dynamics of water droplets induced by detonation waves under varying diameters and Mach numbers were experimentally investi-
gated in the present study. High-speed schlieren imaging was used to capture the detailed breakup process of the droplets. Based on the
experimentally observed flow instabilities, the detonation-induced droplet breakup can be classified into two stages dominated by the Kelvin –
Helmholtz instability (KHI) and Rayleigh –Taylor instability (RTI), respectively. During the KHI-dominated phase, droplets of different
Mach numbers and diameters exhibit nearly identical linear growth in non-dimensional cross-stream diameter with non-dimensional time.
For the first time, significant differences in droplet acceleration between detonation waves and shock waves have been reported. The dimen-
sionless droplet acceleration induced by shock waves is 11 –17 times greater than that induced by detonation waves. Finally, theoretical analy-
sis and experimental results show that decreasing droplet diameter and increasing Mach number can accelerate the instability development
and shorten the absolute breakup time.

I. INTRODUCTION
Detonation waves 1–3 can be characterized as consisting of a
supersonic shock wave followed by a chemical reaction zone, with the
thickness of the reaction zone being significantly greater than that of
the shock wave. The medium at the wavefront is compressed to higher
temperatures and densities by the leading shock wave, reaching autoig-
nition conditions. This initiates a vigorous chemical reaction between
the fuel and oxidizer, releasing energy that further sustains the propa-
gation of the leading shock wave. In the 1950s, Nichollset al.
4 demon-
strated the feasibility of improving thermodynamic cycles using the
detonative combustion. Since then, extensive research on the applica-
tion of detonation combustion in propulsion systems has been con-
ducted.
5–9 As a result, engine systems with promising engineering
applications have been developed. 10–14 Considering the high energy
density of liquid fuels, current research primarily focuses on two-phase
detonation engines.15–17 In two-phase detonation combustion cham-
bers, injectors serve as the primary atomization source, first atomizing
the liquid into droplets.
18 These droplets then undergo secondary
breakup in the flow field, forming finer mists that facilitate combus-
tion. Considering the complexity of the liquid-fuel detonation process,
it is essential to study the breakup dynamics of droplets under the
influence of detonation waves to enhance the fundamental under-
standing of their interactions.
As detonation engines,
19,20 supersonic atomizers, and scramjet
engines advance, understanding droplet breakup has become increas-
ingly important. Understanding droplet breakup under extreme condi-
tions—such as high velocities and temperatures —is crucial for
optimizing fuel efficiency, improving combustion dynamics, and
enhancing engine performance. Advances in this area are essential for
achieving more powerful and efficient propulsion systems. The
breakup mode is one of the key factors determining the final size distri-
bution of secondary atomized droplets.
21,22 As early as 1955, Hinze 23
proposed the relationship between droplet breakup modes and the
Ohnesorge number ( Oh)a n dW e b e rn u m b e r( We). In 1980,
Krzeczkowski
24 further extended this relationship and identified the
transition points between different modes based on Oh and We.

Guildenbecheret al.25 and Theofanous et al.26 have summarized drop-
let breakup modes. Under the condition of neglecting the influence of
droplet viscosity, these modes are primarily classified into five catego-
ries: bag breakup (11 < We < 24),27 bag-stamen/plume breakup
(24 < We < 65),28 multibag breakup (65< We < 85),29 sheet thinning/
stripping breakup (85 < We < 120),29 and catastrophic breakup
(We > 350).25 However, it is critical to recognize that the studies dis-
cussed above focus predominantly on droplet breakup induced by
non-reactive flows. In contrast, investigations on droplet breakup
within reactive flows remain very limited.
In addition to the aforementioned studies on breakup modes,
extensive research has been conducted on the mechanisms of droplet
breakup induced by non-reactive flows. In 1994, Arcoumanis et al.
30
pointed out that aerobreakup always begins with the appearance of
waves on the surface of the droplet. Furthermore, Theofanous26 identi-
fied surface waves as Rayleigh –Taylor (RT) and Kelvin –Helmholtz
(KH) instabilities, suggesting that these instabilities are the primary
mechanisms controlling the aerobreakup of droplets. Subsequently,
Sharma et al.31 observed RT instability at high Weber numbers (/C24 800)
and analyzed the motion of KH waves on the droplet surface, explain-
ing the deformation and breakup mechanism induced by KH waves.
Additionally, they conducted theoretical analyses of the growth rates
and wavelengths of RT and KH waves based on the dispersion equa-
tion of instability. Recently, Chandra et al.
32 categorized different
droplet breakup modes from the perspective of flow instability, includ-
ing vibration breakup ( We < 700), KH wave-assisted shear-induced
entrainment (SIE) mode (700 < We < 2800), and RT wave-assisted
catastrophic mode ( We > 2800). Additionally, they elucidated the
mechanism behind the appearance of RT waves at highWe.
Although the aforementioned studies have elucidated the modes
and mechanisms of droplet breakup induced by non-reactive flows,
research on the mechanisms of droplet breakup in supersonic reactive
flows remains very limited. In 1967, Dabora et al.33 pioneered experi-
mental studies on the interaction between sprays and droplets with
detonation waves, based on the two-phase detonation of liquid diethyl-
cyclohexane and oxygen. They found that the breakup time of droplets
decreases with a reduction in droplet size and an increase in convective
flow velocity. Subsequently, Dabora et al. continued their research on
two-phase detonation,
34,35 discovering that the propagation velocity of
two-phase detonation waves is lower than the ideal Chapman–Jouguet
(CJ) value. They also noted that the velocity deficit becomes more pro-
nounced with an increase in the size of the spray droplets. Similar con-
clusions have also been reported in the works of Bowen et al. ,36
Papavassiliouet al.,37 and Tangirala et al.38 Following this, Niedzielska
et al.39 investigated the effect of water droplets on the propagation of
detonation waves, finding that the inhibitory effect of droplets on deto-
nation becomes more pronounced with increasing droplet size and
number density of the water droplet cluster. Afterward, Gidaspov
et al.40 conducted numerical studies on the effect of water droplets on
the propagation characteristics of detonation waves, identifying the ini-
tial droplet diameter that leads to detonation attenuation in hydrogen–
air mixtures. Furthermore, Watanabe et al. 41 conducted numerical
simulations on the interaction between water droplet clusters and det-
onation waves, discovering that the intensity of the detonation wave
has almost no impact on the final droplet diameter at the end of the
breakup process. The aforementioned studies primarily focus on the
impact of droplet diameter on the speed and structure of detonation
waves, lacking an exploration of droplet breakup dynamics. Research
on droplet breakup dynamics can be found in the recent work of
Salauddinet al.
42 They studied the breakup of droplets under the influ-
ence of shock waves and detonation waves, discovering that the
breakup is significantly more extensive under detonation waves due to
the intense gas dynamics and chemical kinetics involved. This study
shows that droplet breakup characteristics induced by detonation
waves differ significantly from those in non-reactive flows, highlighting
the need for further in-depth research. However, the diagnostic techni-
ques they employed failed to capture detailed information on droplet
breakup, hindering further analysis of the breakup mechanisms based
on the surface ripples observed. Additionally, their investigation of
detonation-induced droplet breakup was limited to a single test condi-
tion, without accounting for varying droplet diameters and detonation
wave Mach numbers. This limitation restricts a more comprehensive
understanding of detonation-induced droplet breakup, which will be a
key focus of the present study.
With the growing interest in detonation engines, further research
on the mechanisms of detonation wave-induced droplet breakup is
needed. Understanding this process is critical for optimizing fuel atom-
ization and combustion efficiency, both of which are essential for
improving the overall performance and reliability of detonation-based
propulsion systems. Thus, the present work experimentally investigates
the dynamics of droplet breakup induced by detonation waves, using
water droplets as the research subject and focusing primarily on the
effects of different droplet diameters and Mach numbers (Ma). High-
speed schlieren imaging was employed to capture detailed information
on droplet breakup. The breakup mechanisms and modes were further
examined based on the observed wave dynamics and flow instabilities.
Additionally, the streamwise displacement characterizing droplet
acceleration was analyzed.
43–45 Furthermore, a theoretical analysis of
the growth rates was conducted based on the dispersion equations of
flow instabilities. The conclusions drawn from this study can explain
the velocity deficit observed in two-phase detonations from the per-
spective of droplet breakup dynamics.
II. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
A. Experimental setup
Experiments on the interaction between detonation wave and
droplet were conducted using a stainless steel detonation tube, as
shown in Fig. 1. The detonation was initiated using a pre-detonation
tube with an inner diameter of 10 mm. To ensure the full development
of the detonation wave, the tube was designed with a length of
300 mm. To mitigate the significant losses that occur when a detona-
tion wave propagates through a sudden expansion structure, a gradu-
ally expanding structure with a rectangular cross section was
incorporated after the pre-detonator. This structure transitions from a
10 mm/C2 10 mm square channel to a 100 mm /C2 10 mm rectangular
channel over a length of 300 mm. The detonation propagation section
comprises a rectangular channel with dimensions of 10 mm/C2 100 mm
and a total length of 1170 mm, designed to ensure that the detonation
wave is fully developed by the time it reaches the observation window.
The observation window measures 100 mm/C2 100 mm and has a drop-
let generator installed above it.
A piezoelectric droplet generator, as shown in Fig. 2, was used in
the experiments. When injection is required, the control unit applies a
pulse voltage to the positive and negative electrodes of the piezoelectric

The process of droplet breakup induced by the detonation wave
was captured using high-speed schlieren imaging. The schlieren system
used in the experiment consisted of a point light source (X150A xenon
lamp), two convex lenses (100 mm diameter), a high-speed camera (i-
SPEED 713), and a knife-edge, as shown in Fig. 3. To clearly record
the deformation and breakup behavior of the droplets, a 200 mm
macro lens was used during the experiment. The high-speed camera
was set with an exposure time of 1 ls, a frame rate ranging from
154 000 to 200 000 frames per second (fps), and a spatial resolution of
32.5 pixels/mm. To monitor the intensity of the detonation wave inter-
acting with the droplets, piezoelectric pressure sensors (manufactured
by PCB Piezotronics, Inc.) were installed upstream and downstream of
the droplet generator. The charge signals collected by these sensors
were transmitted to a multi-channel high-speed data acquisition sys-
tem (SIRIUSI XHS LV) with a sampling frequency of 200 kHz.
The droplet generator, spark plug, and high-speed camera,
depicted in Fig. 3, were triggered simultaneously by a control unit. The
control unit consisted of an ESP32 microcontroller, a boost circuit,
and an output control circuit. The boost circuit was controlled via
pulse width modulation (PWM). The on-chip analog-to-digital con-
verter (ADC) of the microcontroller collected the output voltage of the
boost circuit, and the program adjusted the PWM duty cycle to form a
closed-loop control, thereby accurately achieving the specified voltage.
After adjusting the voltage output to the droplet generator and the
peak time to ensure stable and controllable droplet formation, the
delays from the trigger to the arrival of the droplet and the detonation
wave at the center of the field of view were measured using a high-
speed camera. By controlling the timing of the droplet generator, deto-
nation wave, and camera triggers via software, and accounting for the
measured delays, the droplet and detonation wave were synchronized
to arrive simultaneously at the center of the field of view. This synchro-
nization allowed for the capture of the droplet breakup process
induced by the detonation wave, as shown in the schlieren image on
the right side of Fig. 3.

IV. CONCLUSIONS
An experimental investigation was conducted on the dynamics of
droplet breakup induced by detonation waves, focusing on water drop-
lets at various diameters and Mach numbers. The key conclusions are
as follows:
(1) Droplet breakup process can be divided into two stages based
on flow instabilities: KHI-dominated stage and RTI-dominated
stage. Increasing the Mach number and decreasing the droplet
diameter both accelerate the instability development and
shorten the absolute breakup time.
(2) During the KHI-dominated stage, the non-dimensional cross-
stream diameter increases linearly with non-dimensional time,
with a consistent growth rate of approximately 0.66946 across
various cases. This observation is novel and has not been
reported in the existing literature.
(3) For the first time, significant differences were identified between
detonation-induced and shock-induced droplet acceleration.
The non-dimensional acceleration induced by shock waves was
11–17 times higher than that of detonation waves within com-
parable Mach number ranges ( Ma ¼ 6.49–7.11).
