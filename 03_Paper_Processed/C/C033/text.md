through a two-phase gas-liquid medium

1IUSTI-UMR CNRS, Aix-Marseille Universite´, 5 rue Enrico Fermi, 13013 Marseille, France

(Received 20 April 2011; accepted 4 October 2011; published online 7 November 2011)
We conducted a series of shock tube experiments to study the inﬂuence of a cloud of water droplets
on the propagation of a planar shock wave. In a vertically oriented shock tube, the cloud of
droplets was released downwards into the air at atmospheric pressure while the shock wave
propagated upwards. Two shock wave Mach numbers, 1.3 and 1.5, and three different heights of
clouds, 150 mm, 400 mm, and 700 mm, were tested with an air-water volume fraction and a droplet
diameter ﬁxed at 1.2% and 500 lm, respectively. From high-speed visualization and pressure meas-
urements, we analyzed the effect of water clouds on the propagation of the shock wave. It was
shown that the pressure histories recorded in the two-phase gas-liquid mixture are different from
those previously obtained in the gas-solid case. This different behavior is attributed to the process
of atomization of the droplets, which is absent in the gas-solid medium. Finally, it was observed
that the shock wave attenuation was dependent on the exchange surface crossed by the shock
combined with the breakup criterion.

I. INTRODUCTION
If the aerodynamic forces are strong enough, a water
droplet moving in an air ﬂow can deform and fragment into
ﬁner droplets. This phenomenon, known as secondary atom-
ization, has been widely studied over the last 30 years. 1–4
Droplet breakup can play an important role in weakening
both the pressure peak and the impulse induced by shock
waves. It creates an ultra ﬁne mist which can extract energy
from the shock by droplet evaporation as shown by Adiga
et al.
5 This thermal property of water is also used in ﬁghting
ﬁres in buildings by extracting heat: water either extin-
guishes ﬁres or slows down the ﬁre and chemical reactions,
as reviewed by Grant et al.
6 Over the last few decades, water
sprays have also been tested as a means to reduce overpres-
sure induced by shock and blast waves in view of the fact
that many water spray devices are installed in constructions
to suppress ﬁres.
7,8 They have several advantages: they cover
a large area, they can be used during an extended period of
time, they are environmentally safe, and ﬁnally they are not
too expensive. Previous experiments 7–9 have investigated the
interaction of shock waves with water clouds using a mean
droplet diameters of 1 mm and 2.5 mm, 8 113-220 lm,7 or
micron-sized water aerosols. 9 Whereas Borisov et al. 8 stud-
ied a monodispersed cloud of water, Thomas 7 generated
clouds of droplets with nozzles placed in a staggered posi-
tion, and Hanson et al. 9 used small droplets with diameters
mainly between 1 and 10 lm produced with ultrasonic nebu-
lizers. They found a mitigation of overpressure in the pres-
ence of water clouds. More recently, in a shock tube
environment, Jourdan et al.
10 investigated the attenuation of
a planar shock wave by a well-characterized cloud of water
droplets. In the latter study, they took into consideration the
fact that the typical mean diameter of droplets which make
up water spray ﬁre suppression devices ranged from 200 lm
to 1000 lm.
6 They, therefore, focused their investigation on
three mean droplet diameters of 120 lm, 250 lm, and
500 lm. The attenuation was found to be dependent on the
volume fraction ad, the droplet dimension /d, the exchange
surface area Se, the Weber Number We, and the shock wave
strength. Unfortunately, in these experiments the volume
fraction ad varied with the droplet size. Consequently, a
parametrical relationship was not easy to ﬁnd. Following this
preliminary work, we carried out new experiments to deter-
mine the inﬂuence of the height of a mono-dispersed water
cloud, keeping the parameters a
d and /d constant.
In the present study, we focused our attention on an air-
water medium with a 1.2% volume fraction, made up of
droplets 500 lm in diameter. High speed shadowgraphy visu-
alizations and pressure measurements at different stations
enabled us to analyze the effect of water clouds on the propa-
gation of shock waves. The analysis of recorded pressure
traces shows that when a shock wave propagates in such a
mixture, a situation of great non-equilibrium is induced, fol-
lowed by a relaxation process. Speciﬁcally, in the case of a
liquid-gas medium, the atomization process modiﬁes the
two-phase shock wave pressure history compared to the
solid-gas case. The attenuation of the overpressure peak and
the impulse, induced by a shock wave passing through the
cloud of droplets, are also presented.

Finally, in the case of a gas-liquid droplet medium, the
two-phase shock wave scheme presented above is modiﬁed.
It does not consist of a shock directly followed by a relaxa-
tion zone in which the velocities of the two phases relax to
the “mixture” velocity and the pressure rises directly to a

cess of atomization of droplets generally divided in the liter-
ature into two stages.
4 During the ﬁrst stage, each droplet
deforms, thus beginning to increase the exchange surface
between gas and droplets. In the second stage, the deformed
droplet is broken into smaller ones. The diminution of the
mean diameter leads to an increase in the exchange surface
between the gas phase and the dispersed phase. Moreover,
the momentum and heat exchanges between the two phases
increase with the surface area of the water medium leading
to a diminution of the velocity, pressure, and temperature of
the carrier gas. Therefore, the fragmentation process, which
induces an increase in the exchange surface, leads to a dimi-
nution of the pressure and gas velocity due to momentum
exchanges until the stabilization of the global exchange sur-
face area. When the droplets reach a stable state, the classic
behavior of the interaction between a shock wave and a
solid-gas medium takes place: the velocity and temperature
relaxation processes continue resulting in a pressure rise to
reach the equilibrium value. Finally, as the atomization of
the droplets generates rarefaction waves following the shock
wave, these waves may catch up with the shock wave reduc-
ing the pressure induced inside and after the water cloud.

V . CONCLUSION
Shock tube experiments were conducted in order to
study the propagation of a shock through a well-
characterized cloud of droplets, with two shock wave Mach
numbers, 1.3 and 1.5, and three different heights of clouds,
150 mm, 400 mm, and 700 mm, with a volume fraction and a
droplet diameter ﬁxed at 1.2% and 500 lm, respectively. It
was shown that when a plane shock wave propagates through
a two-phase gas-liquid medium, the induced wall pressure
traces present a particular structure differing from those
recorded during its propagation through a gas-solid sphere
mixture. In general, in such a scenario the shock produces
conditions of the gas which are different from those of par-
ticles, inducing a relaxation zone behind the shock front
where the equilibrium between the two phases is gradually
re-established. However, in the case of liquid particles, the
ability of the droplets to deform and fragment into ﬁner par-
ticles signiﬁcantly changes the behavior of the pressure
traces. The latter present between the shock front and the re-
establishment of the pressure equilibrium a rarefaction zone
which expands and catches the shock wave, thereby reducing
its intensity. A comparison of the times of the rarefaction
zone following the shock front with the characteristic times
of deformation and atomization generally accepted in the
ﬁeld shows a clear correlation and allows us to present this
pressure singularity as the signature of the fragmentation
process. Finally, we highlighted the major role played in the
mitigation of the shock wave by the variation of the
exchange surface area resulting from droplet atomization.
