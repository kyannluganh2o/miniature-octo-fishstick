Fundamental Study of
Diesel-Piloted Natural Gas
Direct Injection Under Different
Operating Conditions
Natural gas as an alternative fuel in engine applications substantially reduces both pollu-
tant and greenhouse gas emissions. High pressure dual fuel (HPDF) direct injection of
natural gas and diesel pilot has the potential to minimize methane slip from gas engines
and increase the fuel ﬂexibility, while retaining the high efﬁciency of a diesel engine.
Speed and load variations as well as various strategies for emission reduction entail a
wide range of different operating conditions. The inﬂuence of these operating conditions
on the ignition and combustion process is investigated on a rapid compression expansion
machine (RCEM). By combining simultaneous shadowgraphy (SG) and OH* imaging
with heat release rate analysis, an improved understanding of the ignition and combus-
tion process is established. At high temperatures and pressures, the reduced pilot ignition
delay and lift-off length minimize the effect of natural gas jet entrainment on pilot mixture
formation. A simple geometrical constraint was found to reﬂect the susceptibility for mis-
ﬁring. At the same time, natural gas ignition is delayed by the early pilot ignition close to
the injector tip. The shape of heat release is only marginally affected by the operating
conditions and mainly determined by the degree of premixing at the time of gas jet igni-
tion. Luminescence from the sooting natural gas ﬂame is generally only detected after the
ﬂame extends across the whole gas jet at peak heat release rate. Termination of gas injec-
tion at this time was conﬁrmed to effectively suppress soot formation, while a strongly
sooting pilot seems to intensify soot formation within the natural gas jet.

Introduction and Motivation
Over the last two decades, natural gas has become an attractive
alternative fuel for engine applications. Especially for large
engines in marine or gen-set applications, natural gas has the
potential to substantially reduce both pollutant and greenhouse
gas emissions. When burnt in a homogeneously premixed charge,
low levels of NO
x and practically zero particle matter (PM) emis-
sions can be achieved. Nevertheless, overlaps in valve timing and
ﬂame quenching of the lean mixture can lead to signiﬁcant fuel
slip of up to 6.7 g/kWh at low load conditions [ 1]. Avoidance of
fuel slip is a major challenge as methane—the main constituent of
natural gas—has a strong global warming potential and can hardly
be removed from the exhaust by catalytic oxidation. Unburnt
methane can therefore reduce the positive effect of the inherently
low C/H-ratio on greenhouse gas emissions and can even lead to
an increased overall impact on global warming. Natural gas direct
injection and ignition by a previously injected small amount of
diesel pilot can prevent fuel slip, and diesel-like efﬁciencies are
achievable [2]. After ignition, the gas jet burns in a predominantly
nonpremixed manner similar to diesel combustion. Therefore,
high pressure dual fuel (HPDF) combustion suffers from typical
diesel engine emission characteristics, but can also beneﬁt from
modern strategies for emission reduction in those engines.
A common NO
x emission reduction technique in diesel engines
is lowering the combustion temperature in near-stoichiometric
regions by means of exhaust gas recirculation (EGR). However,
the reduced oxygen concentration and the inhibited PM oxidation
lead to the well-known PM/NO
x trade-off. Diesel partially
premixed compression ignition (PPCI) aims to retard the pilot
ignition beyond the end of injection (EOI) by lowering the
compression temperature [ 3], which is achieved by reduced com-
pression ratios or by Miller valve timing. As a result, over-rich
regions with combustion temperatures between 1500 and
2000 K—where PM is typically formed—are avoided, while high
levels of EGR keep NO
x levels low [ 4]. The major challenge of
this strategy is increased unburnt hydrocarbon (UHC) and CO
emissions, besides others like load range limitations and high
pressure rise rates [ 5].
Partially premixed combustion is a promising strategy for
HPDF as well, but strongly differs from diesel PPCI in the way
the premixed combustion is obtained. While the diesel ignition is
retarded for PPCI by slowing down the ignition kinetics via
reduced temperature, partial premixing for HPDF is achieved by
simply delaying the pilot injection. Faghani et al. [ 6] were able to
prove a reduction of soot emissions by 90% when the natural gas
jet was ignited only shortly before its end of injection. At the
same time, the strong premixing allowed to escape the PM/NO
x
trade-off, as high EGR rates could be applied to reduce NO x emis-
sions without an increase in engine-out PM [ 7]. Unfortunately,
high EGR and strong premixing both tend to increase methane
fuel slip and therefore have to be kept at a minimum level.
Increasing the global fuel–air ratio toward stoichiometric condi-
tions turned out to reduce unburnt CH
4 by preventing overmixing
[6]. Even though no compression temperature reduction is neces-
sary to realize partially premixed HPDF combustion, Patychuk
et al. [ 8] could show that Miller valve timing further reduces NO x
at high load. At low load, CH 4 emissions dropped by 70% due to
a globally richer mixture with reduced air charge.
This very brief overview of investigations targeting the
improvement of HPDF indicates the broad range of operating
conditions to which pilot and natural gas ignition as well as com-
bustion are exposed, in addition to the changes in operating condi-
tions due to load and speed variations. A previous study by the
authors on the interaction between a single diesel and natural gas

Experimental Setup
High pressure dual fuel direct injection of natural gas and diesel
pilot fuel is investigated on a rapid compression expansion
machine (RCEM) with full optical access through a quartz glass
piston. It was designed by Prechtl [ 10] and Dorer [ 11] and has
been successfully applied to HPDF combustion analysis [ 9,12,13].
Figure 1 shows a cross section of the test rig along with the fuel
injection system and the optical setup applied for simultaneous
SG and OH* chemiluminescence imaging.
The RCEM is pneumatically driven and features two hydrauli-
cally coupled pistons. The opposed movement of driving and
working piston and their concentric arrangement guarantee mini-
mized momentum forces during acceleration and thus allow for
vibration-free optical measurements. When starting the experi-
ment, the working piston rests in its leftmost position and covers
the ﬂow oriﬁce. After the system is pressurized, piston move-
ment can be initiated by opening the bypass valve. As soon as
the oriﬁce is uncovered, both pistons rapidly accelerate. The
working piston reaches top dead center (TDC) and stops when
the pressure forces and the inertial forces within the system are
in balance. As this is a dynamic equilibrium, it is followed by an
expansion. The crank motion of an engine closely matches the
piston movement around TDC, where injection and combustion
are initiated.
Due to the lack of a direct mechanical constraint, the compres-
sion ratio can be varied in a wide range by tuning the driving air
pressure. Different compression speeds can be achieved by addi-
tionally adjusting the driving volume along with the oriﬁce area.
As the combustion chamber walls and the inlet ﬂow are not
heated, the temperature at TDC is a function of the compression
ratio only, while the pressure at TDC is determined by the charg-
ing pressure. Even though this system gives full control over the
thermodynamic state near TDC, the air mass and therefore the
global air–fuel ratio cannot be held constant for different operat-
ing conditions. In this study, the local interaction between two sin-
gle fuel jets is investigated, therefore the global air mass has no
direct impact on the presented results.
The experimental design is based on a previous investigation
on the combined inﬂuence of jet arrangement and injection timing
on the combustion characteristics [ 9]. In accordance with this
study, two separate single-hole injectors for both fuels are
installed at a ﬁxed distance from another as shown in Fig. 1. This
arrangement represents the adjacent jet origins of a dual-fuel
injector centered within the combustion chamber, while allowing
an adjustment of the angle of interaction a between the jets. In
order to keep the conditions for the diesel ignition at a certain
operating point constant, its start of injection (SOI) is ﬁxed, while
the gas injection is shifted for a certain relative injection timing.
All other injection parameters are kept constant and are listed in
Table 1.
In Ref. [ 9], the interaction between the jets was investigated for
operating point OP1 in Table 2. Four operating points are chosen
in total to reveal the inﬂuence of more reactive conditions on the
ignition and combustion characteristics. These are selected such
that the wide range of conditions that may be present in a HPDF
engine is covered. The maximum deviation with respect to the
operating conditions is stated in Table 2 as well. Even though
repeatability is limited, the pressure and temperature history can
be precisely determined after the experiment. As no correlation
between the results and the chamber conditions at a certain operat-
ing point was observed, the inﬂuence of the repeatability on the
measured parameters can be neglected. For each operating point,
spatial and temporal interaction between the jets is varied by
adjusting the angle between the jets a and the relative timing

Fuel injection, ignition, and combustion are observed with a
newly developed simultaneous shadowgraphy and OH* high-
speed imaging system. While the shadowgraphy setup is perfectly
suited to visualize jet penetration and also captures the broadband
black-body radiation of the sooting ﬂame parts, the high sensitiv-
ity of the system does not allow to precisely locate the ﬂame posi-
tion within the dark mixing regions of the jet. Therefore, the
shadowgraphy system is extended in order to decouple the chemi-
luminescence emitted by the OH*-radical from the common light
path and record it simultaneously. This radical is an electronically
excited intermediate combustion species with a short lifetime,
emitting light with a speciﬁc distribution within the ultraviolet
(UV) range during transition to ground state [ 14].
Figure 1 includes a sketch of the imaging system. The UV part
is removed from the spectrum emitted by a mercury arc lamp, and
the light is then focused through a pinhole and parallelized by a
parabolic mirror. The collimated beam is led through the measure-
ment volume and reﬂected back the same path by a surface mirror
within the combustion chamber. Part of the light is ﬁnally focused
onto the shadowgraphy camera sensor by a 50/50 beam-splitter
and an achromatic lens.
If the two mirrors guiding the beam into and out of the combus-
tion chamber are able to reﬂect light down to the UV range, the
OH* chemiluminescent emission from the ﬂame can follow the
same path. A bandpass mirror that reﬂects light between 270 and
370 nm acts as a UV beam-splitter. The isolated UV-light is then
ﬁltered for the maximum of the OH* emission spectrum within a
narrow band of 307 610 nm. The top view of the injector arrange-
ment in Fig. 1 on the right-hand side corresponds to the view
recorded by both camera systems.
Thermodynamic Model
A multizone thermodynamic model of the combustion chamber
is used to calculate the heat release rates from the piston position
and pressure recordings. The model consists of ﬁve zones interact-
ing with each other as sketched in Fig. 2: the crevice volume C,
the gap G between piston and cylinder head, the isentropic core I,
the mixing zone M, and the burnt zone B. It is based on a model
previously reported in Ref. [ 9]. A more comprehensive introduc-
tion to the modeling approach and performance can be found
there. For this study, some necessary improvements to the original
model will be discussed below.
As the heat losses by the compressible laminar boundary layer
were found to be one order of magnitude smaller than the losses
by the RCEMs large crevice volume [ 9], the crevice volume is
deﬁned as the only zone subject to wall heat transfer. The applica-
tion of a constant crevice wall heat transfer coefﬁcient to unﬁred
measurements at different operating points showed that this sim-
pliﬁcation is not sufﬁcient to accurately reproduce the dynamic
behavior. Two model reﬁnements are introduced for better tran-
sient behavior. First, the temperature proﬁle within the crevice is
resolved by dividing it into ten subzones with constant volume.
They are logarithmically spaced, so elements with large tempera-
ture gradients close to the combustion chamber are the smallest.
Second, the heat transfer coefﬁcient of any crevice element i is
chosen proportional to the local thermal diffusivity k(T

Pilot Ignition. Figure 4 shows the pilot ignition delay sign,p on
the jet interaction map for OP1 and OP4. As sketched in Fig. 1,
diverging jet axes are represented by positive values of a, and the

In the case of undisturbed fast ignition at large angles, the pilot
ignites closest to the injector. When the jet axes are turned toward
each other, the gas jet starts to act on the diesel pilot due to the
strong gas jet entrainment. This shifts the point of ignition further
downstream and increases the ignition delay time. The longer the
ignition delay at a certain operating point in general, the further
the pilot penetrates prior to ignition. Therefore, the gas entrain-
ment acts on the pilot over a longer distance and time for OP2 and
especially OP1. At the time of ignition, the pilot injector is already
closed for those two operating points, and the pilot spray is almost
completely absorbed within the gas jet.
Interestingly, ignition in the cases on the border to misﬁring dis-
played in Fig. 6 always occurs close to the maximum distance before
intersection with the gas jet and never within the central part of the
gas cone. When the jets are gradually turned toward each other at any
single operating point,x

tion is inhibited as soon as the free path length falls below the pilot
lift-off distance. Choosing the free path length to be considerably
larger than the pilot lift-off is therefore found to be a good criterion for
stable pilot ignition within the investigated range.

Combustion Characteristics. The previous investigation [ 9]
showed that the shape of the heat release rate dQhr scales with the
degree of premixing for a certain operating point. The degree of
premixing itself can be described by the time of gas jet ignition
tign,g with respect to its SOI. Early ignition results in low peak and
mean heat release rates, and the related long combustion dura-
tions. The time of ignition within the a /C0 t
inj interaction map
therefore describes the heat release characteristics for a certain
operating point and is shown in Fig. 8.
The fastest ignition is achieved when the gas jet hits the already
ignited diesel pilot (positive gas injection timings tinj) frontally at
an angle of a ¼/C0 10 deg. Deviation from this angle results in an
increased degree of premixing, just as advancing the natural gas
injection by reducing tinj does. Due to the region with failed pilot
ignition caused by the long pilot ignition delay at OP1, only mini-
mum premixing is possible at slightly negative angles. For posi-
tive values of a, only a higher degree of premixing can be
realized. At OP4, the pilot ignites earlier and closer to the injector
tip. As a consequence, the natural gas can ignite about 0.4 ms ear-
lier as well within the entire map. Pilot and subsequent natural gas
ignition is successful across the whole map, and the degree of
premixing can be adjusted by the relative injection timing at all
interaction angles.
Even though t
ign,g is a suitable measure to describe the degree
of premixing of a certain geometrical arrangement and injection
timing, the shape of the heat release at altered operating condi-
tions may still be affected, as temperature and pressure directly
inﬂuence, e.g., ﬂame speed, combustion limits, or burn-out. To
evaluate this inﬂuence, the heat release rates at OP1 and OP4 are
compared in Fig. 9 for three measurement points with identical
time of ignition with respect to the gas SOI: minimum premixing
(left), medium premixing (middle), and strong premixing (right).
The curves are averaged among three repeated measurements. For
a better understanding of the differences in combustion progress,
Fig. 9 includes the combined SG/OH* image sequences for
medium premixing at characteristic times. Pilot SOI and ignition
are initiated at different times for both cases, but the two sequen-
ces are synchronized by the natural gas SOI.
Independent of the degree of premixing, the heat release rates
in Fig. 9 show a stronger peak at OP4 in the beginning during pre-
mixed combustion. This behavior may be justiﬁed by the
increased ﬂame speed. However, the images reveal that the peak
in heat release rate always occurs as soon as the ﬂame extends
across the whole gas jet. Due to the fact that at this time the
injected gas mass is equal for both operating points and the overall
fuel consumed is higher at OP4, the consumption of the rich parts
within the center of the jet must be the reason for the stronger
peak.
It is also noticeable that after the end of injection, the heat
release rates are somewhat higher for OP1. This phase can be
attributed to the combustion of the rich zone in the jet core, which
is no longer fed with fuel. For OP4, there is less fuel in the rich
core, as the wider burning limits have led to increased consump-
tion prior to EOI. For minimum premixing, this behavior is very
pronounced. To achieve early ignition, a case with a ¼/C0 10 deg
had to be selected for OP1 ( a ¼þ 10 deg for all other curves).
Thus, the jet hits the diesel cloud centrally and drags it along in
the leading vortex, so that no lateral mixing of the burnt diesel
products is possible during gas injection. As a result, the lift-off
length rises and there is more fuel mass present close to the injec-
tor tip after EOI. The inﬂuence of this lateral mixing can be seen
on the image sequences in Fig. 9, where the lift-off length of the
gas jet is lower on the side facing the diesel pilot.
Since the shadowgraphy system captures the broadband black-
body radiation from the hot soot particles, an evaluation of the
PM formation is possible via the bright areas within the jet. At
OP1, no PM formation from the pilot can be detected due to the
long ignition delay and strong premixing. The gas jet burns with
no visible soot formation as well until the peak in heat release rate
is reached. However, since natural gas is still being introduced at
this point in time, the rich zones being formed are subsequently
consumed in a strongly sooting ﬂame. This is in agreement with
observations reported for both diesel PPCI [ 15] and partially pre-

Conclusions
The fundamental interaction between a single pair of diesel
pilot and natural gas jets for HPDF combustion was studied by
varying the geometric arrangement and relative injection timing
for partial premixed combustion. The goal was to generate a
general understanding of the processes that contribute to ignition
and combustion under different operating conditions by means of
simultaneous SG/OH* imaging and heat release rate analysis.
Assuming that the engine application is based on a single injector
located centrally in the cylinder head, the effect of ambient tem-
perature and pressure on the ignition process was characterized:
/C15 The operating conditions have a direct inﬂuence on pilot
ignition, since they strongly affect the chemical ignition
delay. Therefore, at low temperatures, the adjacent gas jet
has enough time to disturb the mixture formation in the pilot
jet or even prevent ignition. If the ignition delay is low, the
ignition becomes signiﬁcantly more stable.
/C15 An evaluation of the SG/OH* images showed that the igni-
tion of the diesel pilot is not only delayed but is also shifted
downstream when the spatial overlap is increased. This in
turn reduces the free path length of the pilot jet, resulting in
misﬁring when the free path length falls below the lift-off
length for ignition.
/C15 With increasing chamber temperatures and pressures, the
ignition of the natural gas jet is surprisingly delayed. One
reason for this is that the diesel pilot ignites very close to the
oriﬁce of the gas injector, but the ignition can progress to the
gas jet only further downstream. At lower temperatures and
pressures, a joint mixture zone of the pilot and gas fuel jets is
generated prior to ignition due to the longer ignition delay.
This direct coupling results in a signiﬁcantly faster ignition
of the gas jet.
Following ignition characterization, natural gas combustion
was analyzed by means of heat release rates calculated from the
experimental data. Supported by the optical recordings, the fol-
lowing insights were generated:
/C15 Since the geometric arrangement of the injector oriﬁces has
little inﬂuence on the pilot ignition at elevated temperatures
and pressures, it is possible to control the degree of premix-
ing over a wide range of geometrical conﬁgurations via the
time delay between the injections.

/C15 The heat release remains very similar at increased tempera-

/C15 The previous results from other authors on PM emissions in
HPDF engine operation [ 6,7,16] could be conﬁrmed by the
optical investigations. With no sooting, pilot ﬂame soot lumi-
nescence from gas combustion generally only begins after
reaching the peak of heat release. If the injection ends prior
to this peak, soot formation can be effectively prevented.
Furthermore, it could be shown that a heavily sooting diesel
pilot also signiﬁcantly increases the formation of soot in the
gas jet. Although no temperature reduction is necessary to
control the degree of premixing for HPDF (in contrast to die-
sel PPCI), this measure may further reduce PM emissions.
Due to the reduced number of jets, missing charge motion, and
maximized free path length of the jets before wall impingement,
these results may not be directly applicable to full scale engine
operation. Nevertheless, most of the phenomena related to the
ignition characteristics occur close to the injector, where the
abstraction is valid. As shown by the advances in diesel combus-
tion analysis, it is important to gain a conceptual understanding of
the combustion process with a single free jet, before including the
effect of wall impingement, adjacent fuel jets, and swirling
motion. Future investigations may combine these experimental
results with computational ﬂuid dynamics calculations for a
deeper insight into combustion and pollutant formation.
