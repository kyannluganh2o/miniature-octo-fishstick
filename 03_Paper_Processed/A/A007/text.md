Influence of the Spatial and
Temporal Interaction Between
Diesel Pilot and Directly Injected
Natural Gas Jet on Ignition and
Combustion Characteristics
In this paper, pilot-ignited high pressure dual-fuel combustion of a natural gas jet is
investigated on a fundamental basis by applying two separate single-hole injectors to a
rapid compression expansion machine (RCEM). A Shadowgraphy system is used for opti-
cal observations, and the combustion progress is assessed in terms of heat release rates
(HRRs). The experiments focus on the combined inﬂuence of injection timing and geomet-
rical jet arrangement on the jet interaction and the impact on the combustion process. In
a ﬁrst step, the operational range for successful pilot self-ignition and transition to natu-
ral gas jet combustion is determined, and the restricting phenomena are identiﬁed by
analyzing the shadowgraph images. Within this range, the combustion process is assessed
by evaluation of ignition delays and HRRs. Strong interaction is found to delay or even
prohibit pilot ignition, while it facilitates a fast and stable onset of the gas jet combustion.
Furthermore, it is shown that the HRR is governed by the time of ignition with respect to
the start of natural gas injection—as this parameter deﬁnes the level of premixing. Evalu-
ation of the time of gas jet ignition within the operability map can therefore directly link
a certain spatial and temporal interaction to the resulting heat release characteristics. It
is ﬁnally shown that controlling the HRR through injection timing variation is limited for
a certain angle between the two jets. [DOI: 10.1115/1.4039934]
1 Introduction
Ambitious targets to reduce greenhouse-gas emissions from
internal combustion engines lead to an increased research focus
on alternative fuels. Due to the low C/H-ratio and its global avail-
ability, natural gas is a promising option and its share in heavy
duty applications is constantly increasing. When natural gas is
homogeneously mixed with air prior to ignition, overlaps in valve
timing and ﬂame quenching at the wall lead to signiﬁcant fuel
slip. This needs to be avoided as methane exhibits a considerably
strong greenhouse warming potential. Direct injection of the natu-
ral gas prevents fuel slip and additionally entails the beneﬁt of the
inherently high fuel economy of a diesel engine. A small quantity
of diesel injected prior to, during, or shortly after the natural gas
injection acts as a pilot source to initiate combustion.
Compared to conventional diesel operation—where time and
location of the ignition and the subsequent type of combustion can
hardly be inﬂuenced—the process becomes more ﬂexible by split-
ting ignition and main heat release between the two fuels. This
allows for enhanced control of the mixture formation prior to and
during combustion, thereby directly affecting the thermal efﬁ-
ciency and the emissions. In order to tap the full potential, it is
crucial to understand how the additional degrees-of-freedom inter-
actively contribute to the achievable engine performance.
Extensive research on the combustion process was conducted
by a group formed among Hill and coworkers [ 1–5], who used a
newly developed dual-fuel injector for their studies. With mainly
full-scale engine tests, they were able to prove the high thermal
efﬁciency and beneﬁts regarding NO
x and soot emissions when
compared to diesel operation. They considered a whole range of
parameters like injection pressure, amount of pilot fuel, exhaust
gas recirculation level or load.
It is evident that the degree of premixing is mainly affected by
the relative injection timing between the two fuels, thereby having
a major impact on the heat release rate (HRR). McTaggart et al.
included an isolated variation of relative injection timing in their
test matrix and proved its direct inﬂuence on the emissions [ 3,5].
Due to missing optical access, they were not able to describe the
ongoing processes in detail. This gap has been closed by Imhof
[6] and Takasaki et al. [ 7], who directly observed an isolated pair
of fuel jets with ﬁxed geometrical arrangement.
If and when the diesel spray ignites and how fast or efﬁcient
ignition is passed on to the natural gas jet does not only depend on
the injection timing, but as well on the spatial interaction due to
the geometrical arrangement. The earliest investigation on the
effect of jet orientation on the combustion process dates back to
Li et al. [ 8]. They used a dual-fuel injector with a randomly rotat-
ing nozzle and therefore constantly changing jet arrangement.
Even though not directly observable, combustion instabilities dur-
ing tests could be attributed to the geometrical conﬁguration by
statistical analysis of numerical simulations. It is important to
note that the spatial interaction cannot be reduced to a certain geo-
metrical nozzle arrangement, since it in turn is inﬂuenced by the
relative injection timing. Fujino et al. [ 9] were able to visualize a
general dependency of the spatial jet interaction on the injection
delay, and showed that the ignition and combustion processes are
strongly affected.
This paper aims to fundamentally describe the correlated inﬂu-
ence of temporal and spatial jet interaction on the combustion
process by experiments with two single-jet injectors under engine-
relevant conditions. The limits for successful ignition of the gas
jet are formulated, and the ignition process itself is assessed by
the resulting ignition delays and shadowgraph images. In an
attempt to characterize the heat release rates depending on the
ignition phase, a direct link of a certain conﬁguration to the

2 Experimental Setup
Experiments are conducted on a large bore rapid compression
expansion machine (RCEM) designed for good optical access. The
concept is based on pneumatically driven compression and was
developed and described in detail by Prechtl and Dorer for the inves-
tigation of hydrogen fueled diesel combustion [10,11]. Figure 1 dis-
plays the RCEM driving mechanism, the fuel injector arrangement,
and the double-pass Shadowgraphy system applied for this study.
The RCEM features two concentrically arranged ring-shaped
pistons, connected by incompressible hydraulic oil. The opposed
motion of driving and working piston suppresses net momentum
forces during acceleration, thereby minimizing vibrations that
could harm the quality of the optical measurements. Before start-
ing the experiment, the working piston resides in its leftmost posi-
tion covering the ﬂow oriﬁce and preventing oil ﬂow between the
two chambers. The system then can be pressurized by opening the
valve to the driving air supply, but compression is not started until
the bypass valve is actuated. Oil ﬂows through the bypass from
the driving piston to the working piston and as soon as the ﬂow
oriﬁce is uncovered, the pistons rapidly accelerate. Compression
stops when the forces by the driving pressure, inertia, and ﬂow
friction through the oriﬁce balance the force imposed by the
pressure in the combustion chamber, followed by an expansion
movement. Near the top dead center (TDC), the injection and
combustion event is initiated, while the piston motion is very sim-
ilar to the crank motion of an engine. It has to be noted that expan-
sion is affected by the combustion induced pressure rise, which is
small in this study and its inﬂuence is therefore neglected.
The driving system allows to create a wide variety of operating
conditions. By adapting the driving pressure and volume, the com-
pression speed—i.e., simulated engine speed—and the compression
ratio may be altered. Furthermore, pressure and temperature at TDC
can be independently adjusted. As the temperature at start of com-
pression is the ambient temperature, the temperature at TDC is a
function of the compression ratio only. The desired TDC pressure
can be adjusted by a variation of the charging pressure, while keeping
the compression ratio and therefore TDC temperature constant. All
the experiments in this study have been acquired at constant operat-
ing conditions within the repetitive accuracy speciﬁed in Table1.
The fundamental interaction between pilot and gas jet is studied
by installing two separate single-hole injectors for both fuels, as
depicted in Fig. 1 in cross-sectional and top view. While the gas
injector is applied to the cylinder head, the pilot injector is installed
in lateral position. Both injection holes are arranged such that they
interact within a plane parallel to the cylinder head and normal to
the direction of observation through the quartz glass piston.
If a dual-fuel high pressure injection system is to be applied to
an engine conﬁguration, it is commonly realized by a single injector
centered in the combustion chamber. The most relevant parameter
deﬁning the geometrical interaction is therefore the anglea between
the two jets, which is deﬁned as the spatial variable in this study.
The offset between the two jet origins is generally small and there-
fore minimized while avoiding impingement of the diesel spray on
the gas injector tip. Temporal overlap is varied by shifting the start
of injection (SOI) of natural gas while keeping the diesel injection
timing constant for unaltered ignition conditions. The gas SOI is
therefore given with respect to the pilot SOI. All other injection
parameters are kept constant and are listed in Table 1.
Besides conventional pressure and piston position measure-
ments for heat release rate analysis, a double-pass Shadowgraphy
system is applied. This system allows the visualization of density
gradients within the combustion chamber. Many physical phe-
nomena induce such density gradients, like the liquid spray or
temperature and species gradients within the jets and across the
ﬂame front. The Shadowgraphy system is therefore perfectly
suited for the observation of various processes like mixture forma-
tion and ﬂame propagation, while it is difﬁcult to quantify and
correlate the superimposed information to the underlying causes.
Figure 1 shows the main parts of the Shadowgraphy system.
The light emitted by a mercury arc lamp is focused onto a pinhole,
forming a point source with a diameter of about 1 mm. The beam
is then parallelized by a concave mirror with the pinhole in its
focal point. After passing the measurement volume, the light is
reﬂected by a surface mirror within the combustion chamber and
guided all the way back. Close to the pinhole, a fraction of the
light is decoupled with a beam splitter and focused onto the imag-
ing sensor of a high-speed camera. If the light passes a density

3 Thermodynamic Model
To determine the HRR from the pressure and position record-
ings, a thermodynamic model of the combustion chamber is
needed. The most crucial and also difﬁcult point dealing with
such models is the accurate characterization of heat losses. This is
particularly true in the case presented here, as the compressed vol-
ume is large and the amount of introduced fuel is small.
Models based on quasi-steady-state heat transfer assumptions
have been known for a while and are still most commonly used in
internal combustion engine analysis due to their simplicity
[12,13]. They rely on the premise that heat transfer during com-
pression and expansion can be described similarly to heat transfer
in fully developed, steady ﬂow in channels. This implies that the
Nusselt assumption is valid and that the Nusselt number can be
expressed as a function of Reynolds and Prandtl number. Several
authors have shown that the complex heat transfer phenomena
involved within a charge compressed and expanded by a piston
are highly transient, and therefore have proposed corrections to
the models mentioned above [ 14–16].
Besides the fact that they often include application-dependent
model constants, none of these models is applicable to the case
presented here. The main reason is the large crevice volume of the
RCEM (about 17% at TDC), primarily caused by a deep annular
crevice at the piston, and the inlet and outlet bores. These crevices
substantially increase the transient behavior as will be shown
later, especially when comparing experiments with strongly
shifted center of combustion and therefore different pressure his-
tory. Another reason is the laminar boundary layer in the RCEM
due to missing turbulence, which Lee and Hochgreb [ 17] have
argued to be preserved during operation.
The implemented model is similar to the one proposed by Lee
and Hochgreb for a rapid compression machine with a piston crev-
ice speciﬁcally designed to prevent vortex roll-up. They take
advantage of the fact that the compressed laminar boundary layer
can be solved directly from the pressure curve due to the known
boundary conditions, i.e., the isothermal wall and adiabatic core.
The crevice volume is assumed to be isothermal at wall
temperature—justiﬁed by its very thin geometry. This directly
implies heat losses for positive pressure gradients and heat input
during expansion due to negative pressure gradients. Even though
the experiments in Ref. [ 17] are performed on a rapid compression
machine missing the subsequent expansion, a negative pressure
gradient arises from the heat losses after the piston stops.
The isothermal assumption does not hold for the RCEM’s rela-
tively thick gaps, requiring for some inertia in the system. A
straightforward way to achieve such thermal inertia is assuming a
constant thermal heat transfer coefﬁcient in the crevices. This heat
transfer coefﬁcient can be combined with the unknown crevice
surface area, leading to only one model constant. The overall
model consists of ﬁve zones interacting with each other as
sketched in Fig. 2. During compression, the piston crevice is fed
with air from the adiabatic core region, as well as by the boundary
layer consumed due to the piston movement. Heat losses occur at
the crevice surface and through the thermal boundary layer. As a
consequence, hot gas is transported from the core to the boundary
layer. When expanding, the cold ﬂow from the crevice mixes with
some of the main charge in a separate expansion region. This
region is added to avoid homogeneous cooling of the adiabatic
core. The temperature in the crevice can fall below wall tempera-
ture when expanding, leading to heat input. Combustion is mod-
eled by a mass ﬂow to the zone of combustion products, adding
the fuels enthalpy of oxidation. For all the zones, only the species
of air are considered, not accounting for fuel species or
combustion products. This simpliﬁcation can be justiﬁed by the
small global fuel mass fraction of less than 0.3 percent. Further-
more, the fuel is assumed to be consumed at an average equiva-
lence ratio of 0.7. Heat losses from the hot combustion products
are difﬁcult to assess and therefore neglected.

erably tilted to the compression side. An evaluation of the temper-
ature proﬁle at 3 MPa, as well as at the point of maximum heat
losses displays the reason for this transient behavior (bottom plot).

pilot, the natural gas ignition delay is set with respect to pilot igni-

jet with respect to its SOI will become clear in Sec. 4.3.
Not including heat losses from the hot combustion products
results in an underestimation of the heat release rates. This is visi-
ble by the mass fraction burned in Fig. 6, reaching only
0.8 6 0.025. It is therefore not possible to assess the quality or
duration of fuel burnout, while the neglected losses are similar
among the cases and do not undermine a qualitative comparison
of the heat release rates. The generic setup is generally not able to
reproduce the late combustion phase within an engine system,
when wall impingement and interaction between multiple fuel jets
become inﬂuencing factors.

the fuel jet interaction.

gas jet, which slows down the ignition kinetics. Several authors
additionally refer to a direct kinetic impact by altered reaction
paths [19,20].

diesel fuel. For negative gas injection delays, the contour is even

for positive gas injection delays, explaining the arc-shaped con-

4.3 Heat Release Rate Characterization. The focus is now
set to the heat release rates, trying to correlate them by one single
quantity. It is reasonable to consider the time of gas jet ignition
with respect to its SOI as potential scaling parameter (see Fig. 6
for deﬁnition), since it deﬁnes the degree of premixing. To assess
the scalability of the heat release rates, two parameters are
deﬁned: the absolute maximum of the curve as a characteristic
point, and the mean heat release rate for 80% of overall heat
released as an integral quantity. These two parameters—as well as
the corresponding timescales—are plotted against the proposed
scaling parameter in Fig. 11 on the left-hand side. The time delay
of the peak heat release rate is given relative to the natural gas
SOI, while natural gas ignition is used as the beginning of com-
bustion for the combustion duration.
Ignoring the color grouping at ﬁrst, a distinct correlation is visi-
ble for all four quantities. The most distinct behavior can be found
by the time delay for the peak heat release rate. On the very far
left, some values seem to disobey the correlation, but the reason
for this deviation is the second (diffusive) peak being larger than
the ﬁrst (premixed) peak. Late gas jet ignition clearly delays the
point of maximum heat release, while increasing in magnitude
and variation. At a certain point, further premixing results in a
very lean mixture and the maximum rate falls again. Interestingly,
this point seems to coincide with the end of gas injection at
approximately þ2.7 ms (see mass ﬂow rates in Fig. 6). The mean
heat release rate increases monotonically with delayed ignition,
while the combustion duration is reduced. Only for very late onset
of combustion the behavior changes, which is a clear sign of
incomplete conversion.
Now, the cases can be grouped by their time of ignition, as indi-
cated by the colors in Fig. 11 for eight groups across the whole
range. The two plots on the right side include all measurements
for the fastest ignition (top), and the group with the maximum
heat release rates (bottom), along with the average value. The
good agreement independent of spatial and temporal interaction
once more conﬁrms the scaling assumption.
This successful description of the heat release by only one vari-
able related to the mixture quality does allow further conclusions
about the ignition phase. It implies that either the point of ignition
is similar among the combinations, or that initial ﬂame propaga-
tion within the gas jet is independent of the point of ignition. The
shadowgraphs reveal that the location of ignition is in fact similar
when comparing two conﬁgurations that ignite at the same time
after SOI. Within one group, the point of ignition is therefore
fairly constant. On the other hand, the diesel always ignites within
a narrow region if the pilot injection parameters are not changed.
Following this, the point of ignition is only minorly shifted among
all tested combinations.
In a ﬁnal step, it is now possible to directly link the characteris-
tic heat release rate to the jet interaction by applying the intro-
duced scaling parameter. For this purpose, Fig. 12 shows all heat
release curves averaged within the speciﬁed ranges for the time of
gas jet ignition (left). The same ranges and colors are applied to
the interaction map (right). A distinct pattern reveals that a certain
shape of heat release—and therefore the degree of premixing—is
strongly inﬂuenced by the interaction. For example, predomi-
nantly diffusive combustion with a very low ﬁrst premixing peak
can only be achieved by subsequent admission of diesel and gas,
the gas jet being directed toward the zone of pilot combustion
products.
Being a geometrical design parameter, the angle between the
jets has to be ﬁxed and may not be used to tune the combustion
process during operation. A large angle leads to strongly premixed
combustion, while the interaction is low and therefore the gas jet
ignition may become unstable. Parallel arrangement with 0 deg
seems to be a good choice, as it covers the whole range of possible
heat release rates. Yet the pilot ignition has revealed that this con-
ﬁguration is critical regarding stability for simultaneous injection.
Additionally, due to long pilot ignition delay in this region, only
strongly diffusive or strongly premixed combustion is possible.

remain. A small degree of premixing (e.g., to maintain maximum
design pressure at peak load) requires a slightly negative angle
between the jet axes, leading to an intersection of the jet axes at
the point of diesel ignition. However, this conﬁguration does not
allow for increased premixing, which may be desirable at other
load points. Arranging the jet axes under a slightly positive angle

5 Conclusions
An RCEM was equipped with two single-hole high-pressure
direct injection systems for diesel pilot and natural gas, in order to
investigate the inﬂuence of jet interaction on the ignition and com-
bustion behavior. Temporal and spatial variation of the interaction
was achieved by altering the relative injection timing and the
angle between the two jets. By applying the Shadowgraph tech-
nique, requirements for the ignition of the gas jet by the diesel
spray have been formulated:
(1) Successful ignition is possible when jet interaction sets in
below an angle of þ25 deg between two diverging jets.
Due to the strong entrainment, this angle is slightly larger
than the cone angle of a turbulent jet.
(2) When the two spray axes intersect and the injection proﬁles
completely overlap in time, pilot ignition is inhibited by the
gas jet entraining the diesel spray. This is observed even at
strongly negative intersection angles down to /C0 60 deg.
(3) The behavior is inverted when the spray axes are arranged
perpendicular to each other. Simultaneously injected, the
diesel spray is able to penetrate through the gas jet—
forming an ignitable mixture behind the jet. Absence of
temporal interaction on the other hand results in two
detached zones being formed.
Moreover, heat release rates were calculated from the pressure
and position recordings using the presented thermodynamic
model. From the pilot and natural gas ignition delays, the follow-
ing conclusions could be drawn:
(4) Strong temporal and spatial interaction tends to deteriorate
the pilot ignition, while the natural gas ignites faster when
interacting properly with the diesel zone. There is a direct
trade-off between these two over a wide range of timings
and angular arrangements.
(5) If simultaneous injection is desired, the transition region
between slightly deviating and intersecting jets should be
avoided because pilot ignition becomes increasingly
unstable.
Finally, the degree of premixing was identiﬁed to be the param-
eter deﬁning the shape of heat release, represented by the time of
gas jet ignition with respect to its start of injection. This was used

Under actual engine operation, the interaction may be inﬂu-
enced by the swirling charge motion, restraining walls or adjacent
fuel jets. As these boundary conditions are strongly application

appears beneﬁcial to arrange the two jets in a way that they show
a maximum overlap during the injection period for various injec-
tion timings, while parts of the diesel spray should still be able to
ignite freely. As the entrainment of the diesel spray into the gas
jet occurs close to the nozzle where the impulse is large, ignition
is probably inhibited as well for crossing jets and negative gas
injection time delays when swirl motion or cylinder walls are
present in a full-scale application.
