INTRODUCTION
Engine efficiency is critical in maximizing the environmental and  
economic benefits of natural gas fuelling of heavy-duty vehicles.  
Retaining a non-premixed, diesel-like combustion process offers 
high fuel-conversion efficiency and high compression ratio while 
avoiding part-load throttling losses, resulting in efficiencies similar  
to those of equivalent diesel-fueled engines. One such technology , 
commercialized by Westport Innovations Inc., is high pressure 
direct injection (HPDI) of natural gas [ 1,2]. In this system, natural 
gas is injected at high pressure late in the compression stroke and  
burns in a predominantly non-premixed combustion event very 
similar to that in a diesel engine. One technique for improving 
diesel engine efficiencies is through the use of higher injection  
pressures. Some of these benefits have been attributed to improved  
atomization of the diesel fuel spray. As a result, similar benefits 
would not automatically be expected from a non-premixed natural 
gas jet. The work presented here aimed to evaluate for the first time 
the potential power, efficiency and emissions benefits of increasing 
the injection pressures in an HPDI engine from 300 bar up to 600  
bar for heavy-duty on-road applications.
HIGH PRESSURE INJECTION
The advent and widespread adoption of common rail diesel injection 
systems over the past 15-20 years has led to a dramatic increase in 
injection pressures. In the mid 1990's, pressures in common rail 
systems were on the order of 1400 bar [3,4]; production systems now 
offer up to 2700 bar [5], with research systems over 3000 bar. The 
key benefits of these high pressure systems include higher power, 
better efficiency, reduced smoke, and better NOx - PM tradeoffs 
[3,6,7]. They have also enabled more advanced injection strategies, 
including split injection and post injection [8], which are important 
for noise control and thermal management. Higher pressures have 
been shown to generate higher spray velocities and greater 
penetration [9], and narrower spray angles with better atomization 
[10]. In parallel to these developments in the diesel engine industry, 
Westport has pursued the development of common-rail direct-
injection of natural gas technology for heavy-duty engines. Due to 
the added work requirements to compress a gaseous fuel, pressures 
have typically been limited to approximately 300 bar. To date, there 
has been no evidence of whether the benefits of higher injection 
pressures demonstrated by diesel engines, many of which are due to 
the spray formation for the liquid fuel, would be seen in a direct-
injection of natural gas engine.

North America and Australia (Figure 1). Results show that maximum

ABSTRACT
Retaining the diesel combustion process but burning primarily natural gas offers diesel-like efficiencies from a natural-gas fuelled 
heavy-duty engine. This combustion event is limited by the injection pressure of the fuel, as this dictates the rate of mixing and hence 
of combustion. Typical late-cycle direct injection applications are limited to approximately 300 bar fuel pressure. The current work 
reports on tests for the first time at natural gas injection pressures up to 600 bar. The results show that significant efficiency and 
particulate matter reductions can be achieved at high loads, especially at higher speeds where the combustion is injection rate limited at 
conventional pressures. Increases in combustion noise and harshness are a drawback of higher pressures, but these can be mitigated by 
reducing the diameter of the nozzle gas holes to control the fuel injection rate. Higher pressures lead to faster combustion, which 
allows earlier combustion phasing without exceeding peak cylinder pressures, improving efficiency. Steady-state cycle-composite 
results show that efficiency benefits on the order of 3% and PM reductions of 40-60% can be achieved through increased gas rail 
pressures.

HPDI Combustion
As in a conventional diesel engine, in HPDI the inducted charge 
consists of fresh air externally mixed with some recirculated exhaust.
The charge is then compressed; just before the end of the 
compression stroke, a small amount of diesel is injected. This is 
followed by an injection of natural gas through separate nozzle holes. 
The diesel pilot auto-ignites, providing multiple ignition sources that 
ignite the natural gas jets. Assessment of key aspects of the HPDI 
combustion process has been reported previously (e.g. [12]).
In an HPDI engine, end-gas autoignition (knock) is not a concern as 
the fuel and air are not premixed; this allows the engine to retain a 
relatively high compression ratio. The global air-fuel ratio remains 
similar to that of an equivalent diesel engine, with similar 
sensitivity to EGR and combustion phasing. Load control is 
achieved by simply reducing the amount of natural gas injected; 
part-load throttling is not required. Emissions of unburned methane 
are controlled, even at low load, by adjusting the injection process 
to avoid over-mixing of the natural gas. Crevice and end-gas 
regions see virtually no fuel, improving fuel conversion efficiency 
and reducing tailpipe methane emissions.
HPDI Fuel System
Liquefied natural gas (LNG) offers benefits of increased volumetric 
and gravimetric energy density compared to other alternatives for 
heavy-duty natural gas engines [13]. For HPDI, this state has the 
added benefit of being much less compressible than gaseous natural 
gas, reducing the compression work needed to achieve the desired 
fuel rail pressure. A schematic of the key components for an 
LNG-based HPDI fuelling system is shown in Figure 2. In this 
system, cryogenic LNG is first compressed to the desired supply 
pressure (up to approximately 300 bar in a standard HPDI engine), 
using a pump immersed in the LNG tank. The natural gas is then 
passed through a heat exchanger to bring it up to engine temperatures.
The HPDI system also includes a common-rail diesel fuel system. 
This includes a high-pressure common-rail pump, providing diesel to 
the engine fuel system at a pressure slightly above that of the natural 
gas. The diesel rail pressure is regulated using an inlet-metering valve 
(IMV) on the diesel pump. The controlled diesel rail pressure is also 
used to regulate the natural gas fuel rail pressure.
Both high pressure gas and diesel are supplied to the on-engine fuel 
system. The gas pressure is regulated relative to the diesel pressure 
using a dome-loaded self-venting regulator. HPDI injectors are 
twin-fuel concentric needle injectors, with separate needles for 
controlling diesel and natural gas injection through different sets of 
holes. A schematic of the HPDI injector nozzle is shown in Figure 3. 
The diesel needle is located inside the gas needle. The diesel holes 
are located in the tip of the gas needle, which protrudes below the 
bottom of the injector nozzle. Gas and diesel injection are 
independently controlled, with separate actuators to enable changes 
in relative timing and injection durations.

Increased Fuel System Pressures
As in common-rail diesel engines, increased diesel fuel pressure in an 
HPDI engine is achieved by requesting a higher pressure from the 
IMV on the pump. As the pressures used are low relative to modern 
diesel common-rail systems [4], the diesel fuel system is easily 
capable of meeting the demands for pressure increases up to 600 bar.
Unlike liquid diesel, natural gas is a compressible fluid; any increase 
in pressure results in a significant increase in density. As the pressures 
encountered in the fuel system of an HPDI engine (300 bar) are well 
above the critical pressure for methane (the principle constituent of 
natural gas), there is no clear distinction between liquid and vapour

RESEARCH METHODOLOGY
The evaluation of increased injection pressure for HPDI combustion 
reported here includes both computational and experimental 
assessment. The computational aspects include 1-D coupled 
hydraulic/electrical injector modelling to assess performance and to 
specify injector configuration. Combustion CFD using Westport's 
in-house combustion code, designed specifically to simulate HPDI 
combustion, was used to both guide the experimental program and to 
explain the results. The core of the work was experimental engine 
testing on a multi-cylinder research engine.

Injector 1-D Model
The effects of higher rail pressures on injector performance were 
evaluated using a one-dimensional model of the twin-fuel HPDI 
injector developed in LMS Imagine.lab Amesim version 11. Amesim 
provides a multi-physics environment that incorporates interactions 
between the mechanical and hydraulic elements of the injector. The 
model is capable of simulating the response of both diesel and gas 
fuel streams and the actuation of both diesel and gas needles. 
Standard Amesim components are used in most cases, but a 
customized element was developed to simulate squeeze damping of 
the injector plungers. This model used the Reynold's equation to 
adjust the force transfer between the plunger and the needle due to 
the presence of a liquid film between the two elements.
The model is used to evaluate the sensitivities of injector needle lift 
and flow characteristics for both diesel and gas with respect to 
different input parameters. The model was tuned and validated with 
test data collected on in-house custom test rigs under controlled 
pressures and temperatures. The model included the following 
assumptions and limitations:
1. methane was used in place of CNG; 
2. match fits (where high pressure gas and diesel sections are 
separated by closely machined components) were assumed to be 
leak-tight; 
3. the combustion chamber pressure was assumed to be constant; 
4. the electromagnetic actuation was not modeled in detail - 
look-up tables based on test rig data were used to provide the 
relationship of force versus displacement and current; 
5. pressure pulsations in both the gas and diesel rails were ignored; 
and 
6. the dynamic jet forces associated with the flow of fluid through 
the valve were assumed to be negligible.
Combustion 3-D CFD Model
A three-dimensional CFD model was used to simulate in-cylinder 
flows and was coupled with a chemical kinetic solver to evaluate 
combustion progression. The main objective of the modeling was to 
evaluate the local equivalence ratio and the local temperature during 
the injection and combustion event, and to use that information to 
interpret the experimental engine results. The CFD program was 
based on OpenFoam, with turbulent flow structures modelled using 
large eddy simulation (LES). The chemical mechanisms were 
implemented through a trajectory-generated low-dimensional 
manifold (TGLDM) method [15] using detailed chemical kinetic 
mechanisms for natural gas and heptane (a surrogate for diesel fuel). 
A conceptual schematic of the chemical closure methodology is 
shown in Figure 5. Natural gas was simulated with a modified GRI 
mechanism (71 species, 379 reactions) [16,17], while heptane used a 
Lawrence Livermore National Lab (LLNL) mechanism (170 species 
and 1500 reactions). The interactions between turbulence and 
chemical reaction were addressed using the conditional source term 
estimation (CSE) method [18]. The CFD model has been extensively 
validated against in-cylinder pressure, heat-release, and NOx 
emissions data from single- and multi-cylinder HPDI natural gas 
engines; more detail on the model is reported elsewhere [11,19]. 
Particulate matter formation is modelled using the Hiroyasu approach 
[20], with parameters modified to better represent the natural gas 
combustion in an HPDI engine.

Research Engine
The work reported here was conducted on an instrumented research 
version of the Westport HD 15L HPDI engine. The base engine 
configuration and calibration was identical to the CARB/EPA 2010 
certified engine [21]. The engine was installed on an engine test bed 
equipped with an eddy-current dynamometer. The specifics of the 
research engine are given in Table 1. The base engine hardware 
(including pistons and valve timing), air handling system, and 
exhaust aftertreatment are unmodified from the 2007 ISX15 engine 
that the HD 15L is based upon. More details on the research engine 
are presented elsewhere (see, for e.g. [19,22]).
Table 1. Research engine specifications
The research engine was fully instrumented, including six flush-
mounted piezoelectric pressure transducers; two (cylinders 1 and 6) 
were water cooled and the other four were uncooled. Only the 
water-cooled transducers were used for all further analysis. This data 
was collected at a 0.5°CA resolution over at least 80 consecutive 
cycles. The in-cylinder pressure from the water-cooled transducers 
was used to determine the apparent heat release rate (HRR) and to 
define the combustion phasing. This is defined as the timing at which 
half of the apparent heat release has occurred, as calculated form the 
cumulative heat release. Engine brake torque and speed were 
determined from sensors on the dynamometer. Intake and exhaust 
stream pressures and temperatures were recorded at multiple points in 
the system. Charge air flow was measured using a Meriam laminar 
flow element and the diesel flow was measured using a gravimetric 
fuel balance. Emissions of CO, CO
2, NOx, THC and CH4 were 
measured using a Horiba MEXA3100DEGR emissions bench; this 
was also used to measure intake CO
2, to quantify the EGR rate. 
Engine-out smoke emissions were measured using an A VL 415 
smoke meter. These are reported on the basis of filter smoke number 
(FSN), and only represent black-carbon emissions. Previous work 
[11] has shown that percentage reductions in FSN agree well with the 
reductions determined from gravimetric filter-based measurements.
Repeatability and reproducibility was assessed by evaluating the 
long-term variations in measured parameters at a fixed operating 
condition. For this work, the variation in key parameters was 
evaluated for a reference condition of 2400 N.m and 1490 RPM. The 
results are shown in Table 2, in the form of coefficient of variation 
(COV), defined as the standard deviation divided by the mean. The 
results shown are for variability over a period of approximately three 
months during the testing program reported here. This value 
incorporates measurement errors, random variations in engine 
condition set-point, and long-term variations in engine performance.

High Pressure Fuel Supply System
A new natural gas supply system capable of supplying pressures at up 
to 690 bar was developed for this project. This was composed of a 
Hydropac industrial gas compressor to boost the pressure from the 
existing 320 bar test cell gas supply up to 690 bar. A GE-Rheonik 
Coriolis flow meter was used to measure high pressure gas flow.
The on-engine fuel system also modified for the higher pressures. The 
diesel supply system, a Bosch CP-3 common rail pump with IMV , 
was retained but plumbing was replaced with thicker wall tube. The 
natural gas pressure regulation, controlled relative to the diesel 
pressure, was provided by a custom dome-loaded self-venting 
regulator rated to 690 bar. This system ensured that the differential 
between the diesel and natural gas rail pressures remained constant 
throughout the tests, independent of rail pressure.
The high pressure diesel and gas were supplied to the injectors 
through the ISX engine's internal fuel rails drilled through the 
cylinder head. Hydrostatic testing verified that these rails would 
withstand the desired pressure with a sufficient margin for pulsating, 
gaseous flows. The bodies and nozzles of the HPDI injectors were not 
modified for high pressure operation. FEA was conducted on the 
injector to ensure structural integrity under both static and operating 
conditions. All evaluations demonstrated sufficient factors-of-safety 
for test cell research purposes.

Injectors
The two-fuel concentric needle injectors used in the work were based 
on the production version used in the HD 15L HPDI product. The 
modifications for high pressure testing were to the actuator settings 
and the gas nozzle holes.
In the HPDI injectors, diesel acts as the hydraulic fluid that actuates 
both the gas and diesel needles. The pressures in the control chambers 
above the needles are controlled by separate solenoid actuators. To 
account for the higher pressure of diesel supplied to these actuators, 
the actuator ball and seat areas, and the spring preload and stroke, 
were adjusted based on input from the 
Amesim model. To achieve 
progressively higher injection pressures, the preload was increased; 
this led to an increase in the minimum diesel pressure required to 
actuate the injectors. This minimum pressure could not exceed 
approximately 200 bar, as this was the maximum pressure that could 
be achieved during engine start-up. This then directly limited the 
maximum achievable gas rail pressure (GRP) to 600 bar.
As natural gas is compressible (see Figure 4), higher supply pressures 
lead to higher densities. For flow through an orifice (choked or 
unchoked), this leads to higher mass flow rates. Reducing the injection 
flow area by reducing gas hole diameter offers the potential to retain 
the kinetic energy and velocity benefits of higher rail pressures while 
avoiding excessive fuelling rates. For this work, two injector nozzle 
configurations were tested, one with standard flow area and a second 
with reduced flow, as shown in Table 1. The lower flow was achieved 
by reducing the diameters of the gas holes by 20%. The diesel hole size 
was unchanged between the two injector sets tested.
Results
The research program included 1-D injector and 3-D combustion 
CFD modelling and experimental engine testing. Injector 
performance was predicted using the 1-D model to help in defining 
the injector configuration and to understand injector performance.
Injector Performance Modelling
The 1-D coupled hydraulic-mechanical model was used to evaluate 
actuator performance, needle lift and fuel mass flow rate as a function 
of rail pressure for various injector actuator configurations. A final 
configuration was defined based on these results, providing a 
trade-off between minimum injector opening pressure, maximum 
achievable pressure, and good injector control. The results from this 
model are shown in Figure 6 for the standard-flow injector nozzles. 
The model assumes a constant downstream chamber pressure; in this 
case, a value of 170 bar was used to represent the average chamber 
pressure during the injection event. As the flow was choked (pressure 
ratio > 1.8) for all the conditions shown in the figure except for the 
baseline pressure, the downstream pressure has no significant effect 
on the mass flow rate. Even at maximum cylinder pressure (180 bar) 
the pressure ratio across the injector remained above 1.8 for gas rail 
pressures above 325 bar.
As Figure 6 shows, the opening and closing phasing of the actuator 
are not significantly influenced by pressure, but the rate of opening of 
the actuator is increased. This leads to earlier start of actual injection, 
as shown in the mass flow rate plot. For most of the rail pressures 
evaluated, the initial gas injection rate (slope of the mass flow rate 
curve) was essentially constant. However, the higher density of the 
gas increases the maximum mass flow rate. The gas closing ramp / 
time was not affected by rail pressure.
The effects of rail pressure on the modelled performance of the diesel 
pilot injection are also shown in Figure 6. The higher diesel rail 
pressure led to a higher mass flow rate due to a larger pressure 
differential across the nozzle. This increase was approximately 75% 
between the lowest (280 bar) and highest (600 bar) rail pressures 
simulated. The injector also opened more rapidly with higher rail 
pressures. For the injector closing, the timing of the actuator was 
fixed, but the modelling results suggest that, above a certain pressure, 
the needle itself was slower to close after the commanded end of 
injection. This led to higher mass flow rates and shorter separation 
until the gas start-of-injection. This was observed for cases where the 
system pressure was sufficiently high that the force exerted by the 
spring to close the actuator needle was only slightly above the force 
of the high pressure diesel acting to keep the actuator open. 
Increasing the actuator spring preload, to increase the closing force, 
reduced this impact, and brought the actual diesel needle closing time 
back to the level seen at lower pressures. However, this was at the 
cost of increasing the minimum pressure required to open the actuator 
and hence to initiate the injection event.

pressures between 280 and 600 bar. Blue curves are gas injection, red are 
diesel injection. Commanded actuator open times: pilot 0.5-0.9 ms, gas

given below. 
1. Injected gas mass increases with higher rail pressure for a given 
gas pulse width. This is a result of both earlier and faster needle 
lift, leading to a longer injection duration, and to higher density 
of the natural gas.

Engine Testing
The engine testing program focused on three general sets of 
conditions of the combustion process with increased injections 
pressures. These were:
1. “Fixed combustion phasing”: increased injection pressure 
at constant torque, fixed air flow, fixed EGR level, and fixed 
combustion phasing (as defined by mid-point of the integrated 
heat release); 
2. “Constant NOx”: increased injection pressure at constant torque, 
adjusting combustion phasing and EGR together to maintain 
constant engine-out NOx emissions at fixed boost pressure; and 
3. “Increased torque”: increased injection pressure and increased 
torque with fixed air flow and EGR.
Testing for each of the above phases was conducted using both the 
standard and low-flow injector nozzles shown in Table 1. For all the 
tests, other parameters not specifically listed here (including diesel 
pilot quantity and relative delay between gas and diesel injections) 
were fixed as rail pressure was varied. Operating conditions were 
taken from the key modes on the 13-mode European stationary cycle 
(ESC). As the engine calibration for the production 15L HD only 
maximizes rail pressure at high load, the main focus of the work was 
on the high-load modes from the ESC. Although data was collected at 
all 6 modes shown in Table 3, the results presented here focus on the 
mid-speed, 100% load case (ESC mode 8).

To generate a single composite evaluation of fuel consumption and

Fixed Combustion Phasing
To distinguish the main effects of injection pressure on the 
combustion event, the injection pressure was increased while all other 
combustion parameters were held fixed. As the injector performance 
changes with rail pressure, as shown in Figure 6, combustion phasing 
rather than injection timing was held fixed. For this work, the 
mid-point of the integrated heat release rate was used as the 
representative parameter, and injection timing (of both diesel and gas) 
was adjusted to maintain the phasing as rail pressure was changed.

The impact of increasing injection pressure on fuel consumption, 
NOx emissions, and engine-out smoke is shown in Figure 7. A 
significant reduction in fuel consumption (2-3%) is seen between 
approximately 280 bar and 400 bar gas rail pressure (GRP), but at 
higher rail pressures, no further efficiency benefit is seen. NOx 
emissions increase monotonically with increasing rail pressures, 
while FSN reaches near-zero levels above approximately 480 bar.
These results clearly demonstrate the benefits of increased injection 
pressure. The low-flow nozzles can be seen to provide some further 
benefit in efficiency at gas rail pressures above 450 bar, but at

the reliable detection threshold for the instrument. It should be noted 
that FSN will only represent black carbon emissions, and hence 
changes in volatile emissions are not captured here. However, as 
volatile species are usually oxidized in the oxidation catalyst, they do 
not generally impact the PM loading on the DPF.
The slower, lower temperature combustion from the low-flow 
nozzles, suggested by the lower NOx emissions in Figure 7, also 
reduces other measures of combustion intensity. These include the 
three measures in Figure 8: maximum rate of pressure rise, peak 
HRR, and ‘ringing intensity’. These results are based on the pressure 
measured from the water-cooled transducers only. The ringing 
intensity is a measure of the magnitude of the high frequency 
pressure pulsations observed in the combustion chamber in the period 
around the combustion event.
The combustion intensity results indicate that higher GRP leads to a 
more intense combustion event, both in terms of peak heat release 
rate and pressure oscillations after the combustion event commences. 
Interestingly, peak rate of pressure rise does not increase significantly, 
even though the peak HRR increases. This is a result of the fixed 
combustion phasing combined with the shorter combustion duration 
- although the heat release is more rapid, it occurs slightly later in the 
cycle, where the cylinder pressure is already starting to drop; hence 
the rate of pressure rise does not increase substantially. This is shown 
in the pressure trace shown in Figure 9.
The combustion intensity results also demonstrate one of the key 
benefits of the low-flow nozzles. Both peak HRR and ringing 
intensity are greatly reduced, due to the lower combustion rate. This 
lower intensity combustion still provides equivalent efficiency and 
emissions benefits, as shown in Figure 7. This is the principle reason 
for why the low-flow nozzles were run to higher GRPs than the base 
nozzles - the less harsh combustion posed less strain on the engine, 
allowing evaluation of higher rail pressures.

Constant NOx
Increasing the injection pressure while holding all other parameters 
constant leads to higher NOx emissions. This was shown in Figure 7, 
and is a result of higher in-cylinder temperatures. These results were 
based on constant EGR level and constant combustion phasing. 
However, higher rail pressure offers the potential to remove these 
limitations, in particular the limitation on EGR rate due to 
combustion rate limitations and increased PM emissions. To fully 
evaluate the potential offered by higher GRPs, injection phasing was 
varied from advanced, where the timing was limited by peak cylinder 
pressure, to retarded, where the timing was limited by exhaust gas 
temperature. EGR level was adjusted to maintain engine-out NOx 
levels for each phasing.
The results from this evaluation, shown in Figure 12, demonstrate 
that higher pressure has the potential to provide a significantly greater 
efficiency benefit than indicated solely by maintaining constant 
combustion phasing. This is a direct result of the faster combustion 
rate, as this enables the combustion phasing to be advanced earlier 
without exceeding the peak cylinder pressure. (This is demonstrated 
by the later combustion and lower peak cylinder pressure for the 
higher GRP cases in Figure 9.) Simultaneously, engine-out FSN 
levels are significantly reduced due to the higher GRPs.

Increased Torque
The results presented previously were conducted at fixed torque. As 
is typical for heavy-duty diesel engines, peak power and torque in the 
15L HD HPDI engine is limited by the amount of fuel that can be 
burned without exceeding engine hardware limits - in this case, 
exhaust temperature and peak cylinder pressure. Suitable margins on 
these parameters need to be maintained, to allow for changes with 
altitude, ambient conditions, and engine-to-engine variability. For the 
EPA/CARB 2010 certified HPDI engine, peak power was 356 kW 
(475 hp) and peak torque was 2400 N.m (in other jurisdictions, 
ratings on this same HPDI engine platform are up to 600 hp but at 
higher emissions levels [2]). Peak cylinder pressure was 180 bar and 
the turbine inlet temperature limit was 650°C.

Combustion CFD Model
The experimental testing clearly demonstrated significant benefits 
of increased injection pressure at high load. A series of 
combustion-CFD simulations using Westport’s in-house GOLD 
code were conducted to better understand the in-cylinder 
processes that were generating these improvements. The model 
was not modified for higher pressure predictions. Comparison was 
carried out at the 1490 RPM, peak-torque condition. As the model 
work was in-cylinder only, and excluded both friction and air 
exchange effects, the fuel quantity injected was used as the input 
to control power. Injected fuel quantities and timings for both 
natural gas and pilot diesel were matched to those used in the 
experimental engine testing. The comparisons are conducted using 
the standard flow nozzles. Charge mass and EGR level were also 
matched to the experimental conditions.
Looking at the model results in detail allows an improved 
understanding of the changes in engine performance with increasing 
fuel pressure. As discussed above, it was hypothesized that one of the 
key reasons that high pressure provides improved efficiency and 
reduced emissions was the more rapid mixing imparted by the higher 
velocity gas jet. This should lead to a shorter duration where the fuel 
in the gas jet is rich. As the combustion event is mixing-limited, more 
rapid mixing should lead to a faster combustion event. This can be 
assessed by looking at the fraction of the total injected mass which is 
in a rich regime (EQR > 1) at any given point in the cycle. This is 
plotted, in terms of mixture fraction in the rich region (Zrich) as 
function of crank angle, in Figure 14. Mixture fraction is calculated 
based on the local concentrations of atomic carbon, and as a result 
does not distinguish between unburned fuel or combustion products 
[23].
The most striking aspect of the mixture fraction result is how much 
more rapidly the mixing occurs with increased gas pressures. At 
higher pressures, the maximum mixture fraction is almost the same as 
for the 280 bar case, but the residence time in the rich regions is 
greatly reduced. Another feature of the plot shows the much later 
start-of-injection phasing for the higher pressure cases (used to 
maintain constant combustion phasing, and to align with the 
experimental test results). The greater mass injection rate, 
demonstrated by the injector modelling (see Figure 6) is also apparent 
from the more rapid increase in mixture fraction at higher pressures.
The shorter time the combustion gases spend in the rich region results 
in lower PM levels. The PM predicted using the Hiroyasu approach is 
also shown in Figure 14, again as a function of crank angle. This 
prediction does not differentiate PM precursor sources between the 
diesel or natural gas fuels. The results suggest that intermediate rail 
pressures can actually increase PM formation, although further 
increases in rail pressure will reduce it. Oxidation, however, is 
consistently increased with higher rail pressures, leading to much 
lower in-cylinder PM levels at the end of the combustion event. The 
comparison between 280 bar and 455 bar cases is particularly 
interesting, as the peak PM level actually increases for the 455 bar 
case, but much more rapid oxidation results in substantially reduced 
engine-out PM levels (as shown in Figure 7).
The change in bulk average temperature, also shown in Figure 14, 
also gives an indication of the effects of higher rail pressures on 
engine efficiency and other emissions. The more rapid heat release 
rate leads to a more rapid increase in temperature and a higher peak 
in-cylinder temperature. This leads to both more efficient combustion 
and higher NOx emissions.
The mixture fraction plot shown in Figure 14 only covered the 
percentage of the fuel in the rich mixture; pollutant formation is more 
nuanced. A more detailed interpretation of this can be provided by

A cross-section in the plane of the gas jet can provide spatial insight

reduced mixture fractions, as suggested in Figure 14. As well, local 
acetylene concentrations are greatly reduced, indicating substantially 
less potential for PM formation. An interesting observation from this 
figure is that the long injection duration with lower pressure leads to a 
gas jet that continues well into the expansion stroke. The location of 
the piston is significantly changed, so the late stages of the gas 
injection lead to a jet that penetrates directly into the squish region. 
This has detrimental effects on the mixing and air utilization for this 
late injected fuel.
The improved air utilization also leads to better fuel oxidation, lower 
CO emissions and more complete combustion. At the end of the 
simulation, it is possible to quantify the fuel conversion efficiency - 
the percentage of the fuel energy released before the exhaust valve 
opens. This was calculated to increase from 96.1% to 98.6% between 
the 280 bar and 580 bar cases, resulting in a significant improvement 
in efficiency. This percentage is still smaller than the fuel conversion 
efficiency calculated in the measured exhaust emissions (around 
99.5%), due to ongoing reactions and oxidation of the CO and other 
partial combustion by-products. Partially offsetting this efficiency 
benefit is that the higher temperatures in the cylinder lead to higher 
heat transfer losses. To evaluate this, the enthalpy fluxes at the solid 
boundaries were calculated from the reacting CFD model results and 
then used to estimate heat transfer losses to the piston, liner, and 
cylinder head. These results indicated that the heat transfer would 
increase by approximately 15% for the higher pressure cases. This 
transfer is to both the cylinder head and to the piston; the latter is a 
result of the larger gas jet with higher pressure leading to larger 
impingement area, and hence a larger surface area exposed to the 
high temperatures in combustion. This leads to a significant increase 
in heat transfer to the piston crown. The increased heat transfer from 
the more intense combustion is partially offset by lower heat transfer 
over the duration of the power stroke, when in-cylinder temperatures 
are generally lower with higher pressures, as shown in Figure 14 and 
the lower exhaust gas temperatures measured experimentally.
The simulation results clearly demonstrate that higher pressure 
improves mixing, especially in the later stages of the combustion 
event. This leads to significant reductions in engine-out particulate 
and improvements in fuel consumption. Conversely, the higher 
temperature in the combustion chamber also leads to the observed 
increase in NOx emissions and increases heat loss from the 
combustion chamber.

CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
This research program employed experimental and numerical 
techniques to evaluate the effects of increasing the gas injection 
pressure from 300 bar to 600 bar in a direct-injection of natural gas 
engine. The key findings from this work are listed below. 
1. Doubling gas injection pressure, to approximately 600 bar, 
can yield one of a 7% increase in efficiency, a 20% increase in 
power, or a 90% reduction in engine-out PM at peak torque. 
2. The benefits of increased injection pressure are predominantly 
found at full load. Below 75% load, there is no significant 
benefit from higher pressure, as the injection pressure ratio is 
already at a sufficiently high level. Even at 75% load, only small 
increases in efficiency are achieved. 
3. The main drawbacks of increased injection pressure are harsher 
combustion, leading to higher heat release rates and louder 
combustion, as well as increased NOx emissions. These can be 
mitigated by reducing the nozzle hole diameters to regulate flow. 
4. Higher injection pressures can be combined with increased 
EGR levels to keep NOx constant while advancing the timing to 
achieve higher efficiency without exceeding the peak cylinder 
pressure limit. 
5. Injector modelling indicates that the natural gas mass flow 
rates are higher due to the higher density of the fuel. The diesel 
pressure limits the achievable maximum gas rail pressure, as 
injector spring preloads dictate the balance between minimum 
operating pressure and maximum pressure. 
6. The CFD modelling demonstrated that the increase in peak 
combustion rate was predominantly due to the increased 
mass injection rate. Fuel conversion efficiency and particulate 
oxidation were substantially improved through enhanced 
mixing after the end of the injection event, leading to improved 
efficiency and reduced PM emissions. 
7. Over a steady-state cycle, significant efficiency and emissions 
benefits can be achieved through higher injection rail pressures. The 
testing to date has not evaluated performance over transient cycles.
